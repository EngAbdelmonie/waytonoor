"Your First 30 Days" — The Ladder That Leads to the Light, Rung Three: Fasting and Ramadan

WayToNoor — «طريق النور» — "Your way to the Light of Islam — with a companion for your first steps."

Qur'an quotations follow the Saheeh International translation. Hadith quotations follow recognised published English translations, with the same source references (takhrij) as the Arabic edition.

Executive Summary and Prerequisites

This rung brings you into your first Ramadan through the door of meaning, not the door of fear: why we fast, what a fasting day looks like hour by hour, who is excused and how excuses are handled without self-issued fatwas, a realistic plan without frustrating perfectionism, the nights of the month and their final treasure, and what comes after the month. Prerequisite: Completion of Rung Two (wudu and prayer) — for Ramadan is a month of prayer and night prayer before it is a month of abstinence.

Level Objectives

By the end of this rung, you will be able to: - Explain the purpose of fasting (taqwa — God-consciousness) and its spiritual and social dimensions. - Describe a complete fasting day, from suhoor to iftar, in its correct practical form. - List the categories of those who are excused and the limits of the concession, and know when your own situation is a personal fatwa, not a general lesson. - Set your written plan for your first Ramadan, memorise the supplication of Laylat al-Qadr, and choose a light voluntary fast after Ramadan.

Lesson 1: Why Do We Abstain? Meaning Before the Ruling

Lesson objective: That fasting becomes, for you, a school of willpower whose purpose you understand — not a mysterious starvation.

When a non-Muslim hears about fasting, he usually imagines a mysterious "starvation" with no clear purpose. The Qur'an, however, presents the purpose in the very verse that prescribed fasting: "O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous" [Al-Baqarah: 183]. Taqwa means: to place a shield between yourself and disobeying Allah, and for your awareness of Allah to become stronger than your desires. Fasting is an annual, month-long school of willpower: every time you reach for a cup of cold water while you are thirsty, then draw your hand back because Allah sees you, you are training the muscle of "I can say no to myself" — and it is the very same muscle you will need in front of every other forbidden thing in your life.

Fasting also has a social dimension no less deep: when the rich man hungers with a real hunger, not an acted one, he knows in his body, not in his imagination, what it means for the poor man to live his day without food; and that is why charity and feeding others multiply in Ramadan in a way no other month knows. And there is a third dimension, physical and psychological, that many who fast testify to: a whole month of resetting a person's relationship with his body, his desires, his sleep, and his time.

Why did Allah say: "Fasting is for Me"? Fasting has a secret that no other deed shares with it, which the Prophet ﷺ mentioned in what he narrates from his Lord in the hadith qudsi: "Every deed of the son of Adam is for him, except fasting; for it is for Me, and I shall reward for it" — narrated by al-Bukhari (1904) and Muslim (1151). Consider the difference: prayer is seen, charity is seen, Hajj is seen; but fasting — no one knows of it except Allah: you may sit at a laden table and your hand reaches for nothing, and no one on earth knows that you are fasting except you and your Lord. That is why fasting was the purest school of sincerity before it was a school of willpower: a deed with no witness but Allah, for thirty consecutive days. And when He said "and I shall reward for it," He left the reward without a known ceiling; for the reward that Allah undertakes Himself is not measured by human measures.

Three hungers that your fasting trains. Pay attention from the first day: your fasting does not train your stomach alone, but three kinds of hunger, from all of which you abstain:

  • Hunger of the stomach: the well-known one — leaving food, drink and marital relations from dawn to sunset.
  • Hunger of the eye and the ear: that you restrain your sight and your hearing from what stirs desire and clouds the heart; for much of the day's "eating" is done by looking and scrolling, not by the mouth.
  • Hunger of the tongue: the most dangerous of them; for he ﷺ said: "Whoever does not give up false speech and acting upon it, Allah has no need of his giving up his food and drink" — narrated by al-Bukhari (1903). And he said: "Many a one who fasts gains nothing from his fast but hunger, and many a one who stands in prayer at night gains nothing from his standing but sleeplessness" — narrated by Ibn Majah (1690), and graded hasan by al-Albani.

So whoever fasts with his stomach and lets his tongue loose in backbiting, lying and anger has taken from fasting its tiredness and left its fruit. That is why you will find, on your first day, that your hardest moments are not the hour of thirst, but the moment when you are provoked and you choose silence because you are fasting — that is the moment when the school truly starts working.

Sources: The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 183]. Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-'Azim — at the commentary on verse 183 of Al-Baqarah. Islamweb website — materials on "The wisdom of fasting and its purposes" for new Muslims.

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

Answer in writing the lesson's question: What is the hardest thing you imagine abstaining from, from dawn to sunset — food, or coffee, or smoking if you are a smoker, or broken sleep? Write it down; for your plan in Lesson 4 will be built on your answer, and your reading of the fasting verses in Lesson 4 starts from here.

Companion Card (short): The Companion reads your "hardest abstinence" answer and builds the shared Ramadan plan on it later; for the smoker or the one captive to coffee, an early reduction plan is built weeks before the month.

Lesson 2: One Day of Fasting in Ramadan — Its Practical Pillars for the Beginner

Lesson objective: That you see your first fasting day whole, in front of you, from suhoor to iftar, so that nothing surprises you.

The picture of the day, in simple definitional terms: You intend in your heart, from the night, that you are fasting tomorrow for Allah. You take suhoor before dawn, even if only with dates and water; for there is blessing in suhoor, as the Prophet ﷺ said: "Take suhoor, for in suhoor there is blessing" — narrated by Al-Bukhari (1923) and Muslim (1095). When dawn breaks, you abstain from food, drink, and marital relations until the sun sets. When the sun sets, you break your fast, and it is recommended that you hasten the iftar and begin with dates and water, following the Prophet ﷺ, and that you supplicate at your iftar; for the fasting person has, at his breaking of the fast, a supplication that will not be rejected, as came in the hadith narrated by Ibn Majah (1753) and graded hasan by Al-Albani. Then the Maghrib prayer, then a calm dinner without extravagance that would undo the whole purpose of the day.

That is the complete structure. As for the fine details — what breaks the fast and what does not, among medicines, injections, unintentional vomiting, and the like — their reference is the simplified books of fiqh and the fatwa sources referred to; so do not build your fast on social-media posts, nor on the fatwas of well-meaning friends. And a short conversation will certainly happen to you, so prepare for it with a smile: your work colleague: "Not even water?! Isn't that dangerous?" Your calm answer: "Yes, not even water — from dawn to sunset only, then I eat and drink all night long. Two billion people do it every year, and our bodies adapt faster than we imagine. Just pray for me that I complete the day." You will discover that most people do not attack your fasting; they are simply astonished, and astonishment is a door to respect if you meet it with calm confidence.

Your first iftar supplication — say it with understanding. You were told above to supplicate at your iftar; so take now the supplication that the Prophet ﷺ himself used to say when he broke his fast: "The thirst has gone, the veins are moistened, and the reward is confirmed, if Allah wills" — narrated by Abu Dawud (2357), and graded hasan by al-Albani. Three sentences that describe your moment with astonishing precision: the thirst has gone — the day's thirst vanished with the first sip; the veins are moistened — moisture has returned to a body wearied by abstinence; and the reward is confirmed, if Allah wills — a tiredness that has passed, and a reward that has remained. Say it after your iftar with dates and water, in a voice your ear hears; and you will find it sums up your whole day in a single line: a hardship that crossed, and a reward that settled.

Two situations that will happen on your first day, so know them from now:

The first situation: You ate or drank forgetfully. You woke from your distraction with the cup empty in your hand; so do not ruin your day with panic, for he ﷺ said: "Whoever forgets while he is fasting and eats or drinks, let him complete his fast, for it is Allah who fed him and gave him drink" — narrated by al-Bukhari (1933) and Muslim (1155). Hold on to what remains of your day and complete it.

The second situation: You missed suhoor. You woke after dawn and had not taken suhoor; your day is not ruined: your intention from the night stands, and suhoor is a recommended blessing, not a condition for the fast's validity. So abstain for the rest of your day, reassured, and set two alarms tomorrow, not one.

Sources: The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 187]. Sahih Al-Bukhari (1923) and Sahih Muslim (1095) — the hadith of the blessing of suhoor; and Sunan Ibn Majah (1753) — the hadith of the fasting person's supplication at his iftar — verification via Al-Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). Sayyid Sabiq, Fiqh al-Sunnah — the Book of Fasting (a simplified presentation with referral for fatwa).

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

Fast one complete voluntary day before Ramadan (from dawn to sunset, with this same structure) if you are able to health-wise — a full rehearsal that dissolves the awe of the month. (If you have a health impediment, your alternative task is: arrange your whole day around hypothetical fasting timings and write down your observations.)

Companion Card (short): The Companion shares the rehearsal day with the learner in a synchronised fast if possible; he reviews "the hardest hour" so the Ramadan plan is built on it (sleep/work/medication); any precise question about what breaks the fast → a trusted fatwa, not personal reasoning.

Lesson 3: Who Is Excused from Fasting? — The Limits of Mercy in the Legislation

Lesson objective: That you know that the concession is part of the religion, not an exit from it, and that your particular situation is a fatwa, not a lesson.

Among the most beautiful things a new Muslim should know early: this religion does not charge the sick and the excused with what harms them. Allah the Exalted said, immediately after prescribing fasting: "[Fasting for] a limited number of days. So whoever among you is ill or on a journey [during them] - then an equal number of days [are to be made up]. And upon those who are able [to fast, but with hardship] - a ransom [as substitute] of feeding a poor person [each day]" [Al-Baqarah: 184], and He concluded the fasting verses with His saying: "Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship" [Al-Baqarah: 185]. So the sick person whom fasting harms or whose recovery it delays breaks his fast and makes up the days after Ramadan, and the traveller likewise; the pregnant woman and the breastfeeding woman, if they fear for themselves or their children, have their rulings well known among the people of knowledge; and the elderly man who is permanently unable feeds a poor person for each day.

An important methodological note that we commit to across this whole site, and that we teach you from now: determining whether your situation falls within the excuse or not — with your specific medication, your specific illness, and your specific circumstance — is a personal fatwa that a definitional lesson does not own; its reference is your trusted doctor and a trusted fatwa source to whom you present your situation in full. So do not issue yourself a fatwa of excuse as an unregulated easing, and do not issue yourself a fatwa of strictness until you destroy your health; between the two is the path of the people of knowledge.

The excuses in lines — a map of the lesson, not a fatwa. And so that you see the whole map of this lesson in a single glance, here is a table summarising what the text above set out in detail — it adds no ruling, and it does not dispense with your situation card and asking the people of knowledge:

The excuse, as in the lesson's text Its way, in brief
An illness with which fasting harms, or by which recovery is delayed He breaks the fast and makes up other days after Ramadan
A journey involving the known hardship of travel The concession of breaking the fast, with making up the days
Pregnancy or breastfeeding, with fear for oneself or the child Their rulings well known among the people of knowledge — they are asked about, not summarised
Permanent inability to fast (old age, or an illness whose cure is not hoped for) A ransom (fidyah): feeding a poor person for each day
Your own situation — with your medication, your illness and your circumstance It is not in this table; its place is your situation card and a trusted fatwa

A situation card written in front of you, step by step. And so that you see how the "situation card" works in practice, follow a situation resembling many situations: a driver who crosses long distances between cities in his vehicle, who takes a morning medication that cannot bear delay, with Ramadan at the door. He did not sit in his cab issuing himself a fatwa; he walked the steps of this lesson: (1) He wrote his card in full: the nature of his work and his driving hours, the name of his medication and its times, and his usual travel distance; (2) He asked his doctor one specific question: can this medication bear delay until after sunset? And does long driving while fasting harm his concentration and safety? (3) He sent the card and the doctor's answer to a trusted fatwa source three weeks before the month, and the answer came to him for his situation — his alone — not for a general situation; (4) He built his plan on the answer, and shared it with his companion to follow it with him. And notice what did not happen in this story: no decision was built on a post, nor on a colleague's experience, nor on momentary enthusiasm. This is the "path of the people of knowledge" to which the lesson calls you: a written card, an early question, and a decision on clear evidence.

Sources: The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 184–185]. Islam Question & Answer website — answers on "Excuses for not fasting in Ramadan" and "The fasting of the sick person" (in definitional form, with referral of cases to fatwa). Sayyid Sabiq, Fiqh al-Sunnah — the Book of Fasting: the people of excuses.

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

Write your "situation card": Do you have a chronic illness / regular medication / a pregnancy / travel planned in Ramadan? If yes, formulate your question in full and send it to a trusted fatwa source (or through your Companion) before the month — this is the practice: an early question, not late personal reasoning. And if no, write: "I know of no excuse — my plan is the complete fast."

Companion Card (short): The Companion follows up the sending of the "situation card" and the arrival of its answer before Ramadan; the Companion does not comment on the medical situation itself at all — his role is connection and follow-up only.

Lesson 4: Your First Ramadan — A Realistic Plan Without Frustrating Perfectionism

Lesson objective: That you come out with a written, realistic Ramadan plan, and avoid the error of the "perfect Ramadan" that collapses on the tenth day.

A story from recurring reality: a new Muslim decided that his first Ramadan would be "perfect": a complete fast, Tarawih every night, a full reading of the Qur'an, and i'tikaf in the last ten nights. On the tenth day he collapsed physically and psychologically, broke his fast for days, then flogged himself until he nearly hated the whole month. Where was the error? Not in the enthusiasm, but in the absence of the gradual approach that this religion itself taught us.

Your proposed realistic plan: The first and most important goal: fasting the whole month as far as your health allows, without huge side goals. The second goal: attending the Tarawih prayer at the mosque two or three times a week, not every night. The third goal: a short daily reading from a translation of the meanings of the Qur'an — a few pages with reflection are better than a quick complete reading without understanding. The fourth goal: one communal iftar a week, at a mosque or a Muslim home; for Ramadan is lived communally, not alone in a closed flat. And prepare before the month: a sleep schedule that protects your suhoor and your Fajr, a simple food list, and an early agreement with your employer on adjusting times if possible. And remember, in moments of tiredness, his saying ﷺ: "Whoever fasts Ramadan out of faith and in hope of reward, his previous sins will be forgiven" — narrated by Al-Bukhari (38) and Muslim (760). One month with sincere patience erases a whole archive. And a word about the first communal iftar you will live: you will enter a mosque or a home minutes before Maghrib, and see tables being laid and faces of every age and colour waiting for a single moment; then the adhan rises and everyone eats in the very same second, after sixteen hours of shared abstinence. Many new Muslims say that this moment — not all the fasting lessons — is what made them feel that Ramadan was their "home"; so plan for it from now as a celebration milestone in your month, not merely a meal.

A complete Ramadan day, with its hours — a model adjusted to your city. The plan above promised you a sleep and food schedule; so here is a complete day, with hours, for an employee who works in the daytime, at approximate times: the order is fixed, and the hours move with your city's calendar and your season — so transfer the structure, not the numbers:

Time (approximate) Station Note from the plan
04:00 A calm suhoor + intention Protein and abundant water
04:45 Fajr — the beginning of abstinence Then short adhkar
05:15–06:45 A second sleep Protects your day's energy
09:00–12:30 Work: the heavy tasks first Your peak energy is in the first part of the day
13:00 Dhuhr + a 20-minute nap (qaylulah) Recharging, not a long sleep
14:00–17:00 Light work and meetings Reduce friction as much as you can
17:30 'Asr + a light walk Defeats the pre-iftar sluggishness
18:30 Supplication before Maghrib + preparing the iftar The moment of the supplication that is not rejected
19:00 Iftar: dates and water, then Maghrib, then a calm dinner Without extravagance that undoes the day's purpose
20:30 'Isha' + Tarawih, according to your plan Two or three nights a week this year
22:30 Early sleep Tomorrow's suhoor is built from here

Two rules preserve it from collapse: sleep comes in two blocks, not one torn one, and your hardest tasks are in the first part of the day. And if your work differs (night shifts, for example), build your day with your companion on the same pattern: a suhoor before your dawn, protected sleep, and a weekly communal iftar.

Sources: Sahih Al-Bukhari (38) and Sahih Muslim (760) — the hadith of the virtue of fasting Ramadan out of faith and in hope of reward — verification via Al-Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). Islamweb website — guides on "The new Muslim's first Ramadan." Islam Question & Answer website — answers on preparing for Ramadan for new Muslims.

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

(1) Write your plan: a proposed sleep and food schedule for the first week of Ramadan, with your four goals adjusted to your circumstance; (2) Read the fasting verses [Al-Baqarah: 183–185] in a translation of the meanings and write down three meanings that touched you; (3) Choose a fasting companion or support group (a mosque / a centre / the project's internal community) and introduce yourself to them before the month.

Companion Card (short): The written plan is approved in a joint meeting before Ramadan (it is the level's official "shared Ramadan plan"); in the month itself: lighter, closer follow-up, and one weekly question: "What was the hardest day? And why?"

Lesson 5: The Nights of Ramadan — Tarawih and Laylat al-Qadr, in a Language of Love

Lesson objective: That you know the treasure of the month's nights so it does not pass you by, and that you memorise the supplication of Laylat al-Qadr from now.

Ramadan has nights unlike any other nights. After 'Isha', Muslims gather in the mosques for the Tarawih prayer: a long night prayer in which parts of the entire Qur'an are recited across the month, with the voices of the imams, the humility of the worshippers, and a spirituality unlike anything else in the whole year. You are not required to complete it in full from your first year; pray what you are able to, sit, listen, and drink from the atmosphere.

And in the last ten nights of the month there is a single night better than a thousand months — more than eighty-three years of a lifetime — Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Decree, in which the Qur'an was sent down: "The Night of Decree is better than a thousand months" [Al-Qadr: 3]. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever stands in prayer on Laylat al-Qadr out of faith and in hope of reward, his previous sins will be forgiven" — narrated by Al-Bukhari (1901) and Muslim (760). And 'A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, asked the Prophet ﷺ: If I come upon Laylat al-Qadr, what should I say? He said: "Say: Allahumma innaka 'afuwwun tuhibbu al-'afwa fa'fu 'anni (O Allah, You are Pardoning and You love pardon, so pardon me)" — narrated by Al-Tirmidhi (3513) and Ibn Majah (3850) and authenticated by Al-Albani. A supplication of seven words that may be the most precious thing you say in your whole year; memorise it from now.

The odd nights on your calendar. And how do you find Laylat al-Qadr in practice? He ﷺ said: "Seek Laylat al-Qadr in the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan" — narrated by al-Bukhari (2017) and Muslim (1169). The odd: the odd-numbered nights of the last ten — 21, 23, 25, 27 and 29, according to the month's entry in your country; and for this reason your Night 27 may differ from your brother's, in a country that began fasting a day after you, so do not build your plan on a circulated Gregorian date, but on counting the nights of your own month. And a plan for one who cannot stay up all night — a minimum programme for each odd night: the 'Isha' and Tarawih prayers, even with a few rak'ahs, then half an hour of Qur'an and supplication, with your memorised supplication and a written list of needs, and a small charity, even the value of a coffee, then early sleep that helps you for tomorrow's night. Five nights at this sincere measure are better than one exhausted night followed by nights in which you sleep through them all. And set a reminder on your phone from the twentieth day: five odd nights are coming, each with its small programme ready — for whoever entered the last ten with his plan written did not waste his first night in planning.

And your night may be in your home. And if night work, an illness, or small children keep you from the mosque, Laylat al-Qadr does not live in the mosques alone. Make in your home a fixed "standing corner" throughout the ten: a prayer mat in a quiet place, your mushaf, a written supplication list with the names of those you love and needs you know, and a phone switched off, outside the room. And stand in it, after 'Isha' or before Fajr, with what is easy — for two rak'ahs with a surah you have memorised are better than zero — and make the supplication "Allahumma innaka 'afuwwun tuhibbu al-'afwa fa'fu 'anni" the refrain of every prostration. Many stood the greatest nights of their lives in a small room no one knew of; for the night is attained by sincerity, not by the address. And tell your companion about your corner; for some nights are stood communally, even with an exchanged message of encouragement before the standing.

Sources: The Noble Qur'an: Surah Al-Qadr [97: 1–5]. Sahih Al-Bukhari (1901) and Sahih Muslim (760) — the hadith of standing in prayer on Laylat al-Qadr; and Sunan Al-Tirmidhi (3513) — the supplication of Laylat al-Qadr — verification via Al-Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). Islamweb website — materials on the virtues of the last ten nights and Laylat al-Qadr.

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

Memorise the supplication of Laylat al-Qadr now ("Allahumma innaka 'afuwwun tuhibbu al-'afwa fa'fu 'anni") and repeat it in your prostration this week; and plan in your notebook: which Tarawih nights will you attend this week/month?

Companion Card (short): In the last ten nights: one short message of encouragement on the odd-numbered nights, without insistence; whoever makes i'tikaf or lengthens his standing is celebrated, and whoever falls short for an excuse is not blamed.

Lesson 6: After Ramadan — Voluntary Fasting for Whoever Loved More

Lesson objective: That you carry the fitness of willpower with you for the rest of the year, with a light, lasting fast — not with seasonal enthusiasm that dies out.

When your first Ramadan ends successfully, you will feel a strange paradox: sorrow at the month's departure despite its hardship. That is a sign the school succeeded. And for whoever wants to preserve the fitness of willpower for the rest of the year, the Sunnah has opened light doors: fasting Mondays and Thursdays of every week, fasting three days of every lunar month (the white days: 13, 14, and 15), and fasting six days of Shawwal after Ramadan, of which the Prophet ﷺ said that they, together with Ramadan, are like fasting the whole of time — as in Sahih Muslim (1164). All of it is voluntary, not obligatory; take from it what keeps your heart alive and does not exhaust your body, and remember the path's rule: the most lasting deeds, even if small, are more beloved to Allah than seasons of enthusiasm that die out. And preserve from your first Ramadan at least one written memory — the hardest day, the most beautiful iftar, a moment in which you felt the sky was near — for you will read it before your second Ramadan and marvel at how far you have travelled.

Your first week of voluntary fasting — day by day. And so that your choice in this lesson's task does not remain a foggy intention, here is the plan of your first week after Ramadan, drawn day by day. We have assumed that your chosen fasting day is Monday — an established Sunnah and a practical start to the week — and you may swap the day for what suits your schedule:

Day Its task
Saturday Decision and preparation: fix your fasting day, tell your Companion, and prepare a light suhoor
Sunday Early sleep, and an ordinary dinner without "compensatory" exaggeration
Monday Your first fasting day: a simple suhoor, an ordinary day with no heroics, and an ordinary iftar — then one line in your log: the hardest hour? And the most beautiful moment?
Tuesday An ordinary day; read yesterday's line and decide: does this day become weekly?
Wednesday An ordinary day; and if you want a second day, Thursday is the candidate — do not bind yourself to it yet
Thursday An optional second day, or a light review day: ten minutes in which you re-read your written Ramadan memories
Friday Review of the week with your Companion: what has become established? And what is to be lightened?

And the rule governing this whole week: its success is not measured by the number of days, but by its ending while you are able to repeat it; for one week completed sincerely builds a habit, while two weeks of collapsed enthusiasm build aversion.

The six of Shawwal and the white days on your calendar. And two small tools remain that make your voluntary fasting schedulable, not mood-based. The first — the six of Shawwal: do not wait to fast them all in one go at the start of the month, making it heavy on you immediately after your Eid; spread them over the weeks of Shawwal — a day or two each week — so they become part of your rhythm, not a separate project. And the second — the white days: set a recurring monthly alert on your phone for days 13, 14 and 15 of every Hijri month in your city's calendar; for what kills voluntary fasting most is not hardship, but forgetting the appointment until it has passed.

Sources: Sahih Muslim (1164) — the hadith of fasting the six days of Shawwal — verification via Al-Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). Islam Question & Answer website — answers on voluntary fasting for new Muslims. Islamweb website — the Book of Fasting for beginners.

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

Choose one realistic voluntary form for after Ramadan (one day a week, or the white days monthly, or the six of Shawwal) and write it in your plan with your Companion — a small written commitment is better than a large, foggy intention.

Companion Card (short): The chosen voluntary form is reviewed after its first month of implementation; a long interruption is a signal to review the plan, not a signal for blame.

Completing Rung Three: The Completion Standard and the Celebration Moment

Completion standard: This level is complete when the average of its lesson quizzes reaches at least 80% — attempts are taken in sequence, each new attempt unlocked only after a guided review task is completed, and the best attempt counts — together with completion of all the level's practice tasks: a trial fasting day (or its documented alternative), a situation card sent if an excuse exists, a written Ramadan plan approved with the Companion, the supplication of Laylat al-Qadr memorised, and a voluntary form chosen. No certificate is issued for completing this level; instead, its rung on the ladder lights up, and the next rung opens. Achievement mark (not a completion certificate): A third lit rung on the ladder that leads to the Light + the message "You have reached the third rung" + your Companion's closing review, and the opening of Rung Four. Exit checklist for this rung (in the Kawthar style):

Celebration moment text (shown on completion): "Your third rung is lit: you knew why you fast, you saw your day whole before you lived it, you wrote your plan without illusions, and you memorised the supplication of the best night of a lifetime. Your first Ramadan is no longer an unknown that frightens you — it has become a guest you are preparing for. And your fasting Companion is with you in it, day by day." You have reached the third rung

Your third rung is lit: you knew why you fast, you saw your day whole before you lived it, you wrote your plan without illusions, and you memorised the supplication of the best night of a lifetime. Your first Ramadan is no longer an unknown that frightens you — it has become a guest you are preparing for. And your fasting Companion is with you in it, day by day.

General Sources List for the Level (Compilation)

  1. The Noble Qur'an — the verses: [Al-Baqarah: 183–187], Surah Al-Qadr [97: 1–5].
  2. Sahih Al-Bukhari (1923, 38, 1901) and Sahih Muslim (1095, 760, 1164) and Sunan Ibn Majah (1753) and Al-Tirmidhi (3513) — verification via Al-Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net).
  3. Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-'Azim.
  4. Sayyid Sabiq, Fiqh al-Sunnah — the Book of Fasting.
  5. Islamweb website (islamweb.net) — Ramadan guides for the new Muslim.
  6. Islam Question & Answer website (islamqa.info) — answers on fasting and its excuses.

Methodological note for the participant: The lessons of this level are a general introduction to fasting Ramadan and preparing for it, and are not a fatwa in a health or personal situation; for your particular situation, consult your doctor and a trusted fatwa source.
