1. Who Is This Visitor? And What Does He Actually Believe?

Buddhism — as its earliest texts describe it — is a path of liberation from suffering, not a system of worship of a creator god. In the first sermon attributed to the Buddha (the Sermon Setting in Motion the Wheel of Dhamma, in the Samyutta Nikaya 56.11), by meaning, the Four Noble Truths are set out: that there is suffering and incompleteness in life (dukkha), that suffering has an origin — clinging and craving, that the extinction of clinging is the extinction of suffering, and that the way to that is the Noble Eightfold Path (right understanding, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration). And at the heart of the doctrine: anatta — no fixed, independent self/soul; dependent origination — everything arises through conditions and ceases with their ceasing; karma and rebirth — the fruits of deeds across a cycle of births; and the goal, nirvana: the extinction of the cycle and liberation from it.

And the schools branch out: Theravada (the oldest, in Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia) holds to the Pali texts and to the goal of individual liberation; Mahayana (East Asia) adds the ideal of the bodhisattva — who delays his own liberation to help all beings — and meditations on "emptiness" (shunyata); and Vajrayana (Tibet and Mongolia) adds devotional practices and rich rituals. And the Buddha himself, for his followers, is an awakened teacher who woke himself and proclaimed the path — not a creator god who is worshipped. And the statues, for them, are means of reminder and focus and places of reverence for the teacher and his example — and many Buddhists immediately correct any description of them as "gods"; whoever gets this classification wrong in the first minute has lost the whole conversation (this doorway's first rule).

This visitor does not set out from the question "Who created the universe?" but from the question "How does suffering end?" His questions: What does a "God" add to a path that already treats pain? And why one life and an eternal judgement — is that not an injustice to one born into harsh circumstances? And is clinging to a God and to His Paradise not another form of the very clinging the path teaches us to leave?

2. Entry Questions and Hooks

  • "Our path works: I am calmer and more compassionate than I was. Why do I need a god?"
  • "Your God watches and reckons; with us no one watches — the moral law works by itself (karma)."
  • "Do you not see that the promise of Paradise is clinging in disguise?"

The alternative opening for the lead unit (3) — its proposed verbatim text:

"Your first Noble Truth is that there is suffering in life. And the first thing our Book says about the human being: 'We have certainly created man into hardship' [Al-Balad: 4] — into toil and struggle. We do not disagree with you that pain is the first question; we differ — calmly, and without asking anything of you now — on the diagnosis and on the end of the path. This unit walks with you through the question of pain from its beginning: where did it come from? And does it end with the extinction of desire alone? Or is there, behind the law your path describes… a Lawgiver?"

3. Bridges to Islam

  1. Real moral ground: Compassion (his central virtue), generosity, self-restraint and refraining from harm — his fivefold ethical system (refraining from killing, stealing, lying, intoxicants…) meets Islam's ethics across a wide meeting ground (Unit 9 is its natural ground).
  2. The value of contemplation and presence: What he practises as mindfulness and presence has, with us, a living counterpart: remembrance (dhikr) and prayer as presence, not as a bare ritual (Unit 7 in its place in his path).
  3. A relative detachment from consumption and from clinging to appearances: A shared sensitivity against the worship of matter — Unit 3 is built upon it.
  4. That deeds have moral fruits: An honest partial overlap with reckoning — without claiming identity: with him, an impersonal law; with us, a personal, merciful justice; and the difference itself is the subject of the conversation, not its obstacle.
  5. The path's fruits in the soul: Our bridge here is experiential/behavioural — the path's tranquillity is respected and measured, and then the question of the foundation is opened.

"An eternal judgement on one life is an injustice" Presenting justice with us as it is: graduated, preceded by mercy, with repentance open inside life itself, and responsibility only for what the soul itself earned: "Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity" [Al-Baqarah: 286]; and a calm comparison with the justice of rebirth: responsibility for sins their owner does not remember, across births whose number he does not know — which of the two pictures is closer to the justice he seeks? "Islam is another clinging — to Paradise and to God" A calm, non-defensive distinction between the goal of worship (nearness to God and knowing Him) and its fruit (Paradise is a blessing, not a commodity worshipped for its own sake); and presenting worship as love, not a bargain Unit 3 + Unit 2 (Allah's names: Al-Wadud, the Most Loving; Al-Qarib, the Near)

5. The Path: The Lead Unit and the Presentation Order

This doorway's complete order: 3 ← 1 ← 2 ← 9 ← 11 ← 8 ← 6 ← 5 ← 7 (prayer as presence and remembrance) ← 4 ← 10 ← 12.

  • Emphasis: Tranquillity, compassion, the fruits of daily discipline; a slow pace and sessions longer than average.

  • The one next step on his entry (B12): "The question of pain from its beginning" — Unit 3 with this doorway's opening.
