Unit Two — The One Allah: Who Is Allah in Islam?

WayToNoor — «طريق النور» — "Your way to the Light of Islam — with a companion for your first steps."

Qur'an quotations follow the Saheeh International translation. Hadith quotations follow recognised published English translations, with the same source references (takhrij) as the Arabic edition.

1. Opening Hook

Pause for a moment at the first word you most likely heard in your life about Islam: "Allah." Perhaps you heard it in a news bulletin, in a heated debate, or in a film that draws a ready-made picture. But have you ever heard how Allah presents Himself, by Himself? This unit does not begin from our words; it begins from His words — and you will discover that the picture is far more beautiful, closer, and more merciful than what was drawn for you.

2. Executive Summary

Allah in Islam is One, One alone, with no partner and no equal, the Creator and Ordainer of everything, resembling none of His creatures in anything; and with His complete transcendence, He is Merciful and Near, answering whoever calls upon Him and decreeing mercy upon Himself. And "Allah" is not the name of a god specific to Muslims; it is the name of Allah in Arabic among Arab Muslims, Christians, and Jews alike. And the human being knows his Lord, for Muslims, by the mind looking into creation and by revelation completing it, together. This is the picture the Qur'an presents of Allah, and the sections of this unit detail it one by one, from its first sources.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 2 of 12 in the path "Get to Know Islam." It is recommended that you have read Unit One, "What Is Islam? And Why Does the Human Being Search for the Truth?", for in it the meaning of the word "Islam" and the method of this whole path are founded; but this unit stands on its own for whoever reached it directly. And it is the cornerstone upon which all the following units are built: our talk about purpose, the prophets, the Qur'an, and the pillars assumes first that you know who Allah is, to whom Islam calls.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: - Explain, in your own words, the meaning of Tawhid as the Qur'an presents it, and why Islam insists upon it. - Describe how Allah presents Himself in the Qur'an: One, transcendent above resembling His creation, Merciful, Near. - Distinguish between the name "Allah" in Arabic and the claim of "a god specific to Muslims." - Show the two means of knowledge of Allah in the Islamic conception: reason and revelation, and how they complement one another.

5. Main Text

5.1 A Question with Which Everything Must Begin: Who Is Allah?

If Islam is a call to worship Allah alone, the first fair question for you to ask is: Who is this Allah to whom you call me? And is He as I heard about Him in the news and the debates, or is the real picture different?

Our method in this unit is simple and direct: we will not introduce Allah to you from our own words, but from His words about Himself in the Qur'an, and from the words of His Prophet ﷺ about Him. For the fairest way to judge any conception is to hear it from its owner first, not from its opponents. And you will quickly discover that the Qur'anic picture of Allah is far more beautiful, closer, and more merciful than what the distorted images promote: One God, with no partner and no equal, who created everything, has mercy on everything, hears everyone, and needs no one.

Let us begin from the shortest surah in the Qur'an and the greatest of them in this door — a surah Muslims called "Al-Ikhlas" (Sincerity) because it devoted Tawhid purely to its Owner: "Say, 'He is Allah, [who is] One, Allah, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, Nor is there to Him any equivalent'" [Al-Ikhlas: 1–4]. Four short verses carrying a complete creed: Allah is One, One alone, indivisible and not multiple; Samad — that is, a complete Master whom creation seeks for their needs while He is free of need of them; He neither begets nor is born, so He has neither a beginning nor offspring like His creatures; and He has no equal and no peer in anything. This small surah equals, for Muslims, a third of the Qur'an in its subject, because it is the core of what all the messages came with.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: Surah Al-Ikhlas [112: 1–4] in full. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of Surah Al-Ikhlas, the reason for its name, and its virtue. - Islam Question & Answer website (islamqa.info) — answers to "Who is Allah?", directed to non-Muslims.

5.2 One, with No Partner: Why Does Islam Insist on Tawhid?

The whole heart of Islam is one word: Tawhid. That Allah is One in His essence, His attributes, and His actions, and One in His deserving worship alone. A reader may wonder: why all this insistence? Is it not enough that a person worship a good god, whoever he may be?

Islam answers from two angles. The angle of reason: this universe, organised by single, coherent laws — from the movement of the planets to the heartbeat of the foetus — points to One Ordainer; and if the ordaining wills were multiple, they would have collided and contended, as He the Exalted said: "Had there been within the heavens and earth gods besides Allah, they both would have been ruined. So exalted is Allah, Lord of the Throne, above what they describe" [Al-Anbiya: 22]. And the angle of the heart: the human being whose loyalties and worship are distributed among gods, idols, people, and desires lives torn, settling upon no single master who pleases him; whereas whoever unified his direction, gathering his heart upon One Lord, has found the compass that does not change. He the Exalted said: "Allah presents an example: a slave owned by quarreling partners and another belonging exclusively to one man — are they equal in comparison? Praise be to Allah! But most of them do not know" [Az-Zumar: 29].

That is why the first words in the adhan that a Muslim hears five times a day are "Allah is the Greatest" and "There is no god but Allah," and why the Shahada by which a person enters Islam is a declaration of this Tawhid before anything else. For Tawhid in Islam is not a theoretical matter in books of creed; it is a daily liberation of the human being: he fears none but Allah, hopes in none but Allah, and his heart bows to no creature like him.

5.3 He Resembles Nothing of His Creation

One of the most common distorted images is that Allah — in any religion — is imagined in the image of a large human being sitting somewhere in the sky, with the limited moods and anger of human beings and their limits. The Qur'an, however, cuts this conception off at its root with a decisive verse: "There is nothing like unto Him, and He is the Hearing, the Seeing" [Ash-Shura: 11]. For Allah, for Muslims, resembles His creatures neither in His essence nor in His attributes; whatever images cross your mind, Allah is other than that, because He is the Creator of all images, and the creator of a thing is not of the genus of what he makes — just as the carpenter is not of the genus of the wood from which he makes the door. And He the Exalted says about Himself: "Lord of the heavens and the earth and whatever is between them — so worship Him and have patience for His worship. Do you know of any similarity to Him?" [Maryam: 65] — Do you know of any equal or peer for Him? A question whose known answer is: No.

And with this complete transcendence, Islam does not make its Lord obscure and unknown; rather, it introduces Him by His Names and Attributes that He mentioned in His Book: He is Ar-Rahman (the Most Merciful), Ar-Rahim (the Especially Merciful), Al-Alim (the All-Knowing), Al-Hakim (the All-Wise), Al-Adl (the Just), Al-Hakam (the Judge), Al-Ghafoor (the All-Forgiving), Al-Wadud (the Most Loving), Al-Qarib (the Near), Al-Mujib (the Responsive). Muslims affirm for Allah what He affirmed for Himself of complete attributes, without likening them to the attributes of creatures; His hearing is not like our hearing, His sight is not like our sight, and His mercy is not like our fluctuating emotions. And thus the Islamic conception combines two matters whose combination is difficult in religions and philosophies: a God transcendent, who cannot be delimited or pictured, and a God known by His Names, near by His actions and His mercy.

That is why Muslims make for Allah no statue, image, or icon; for the One who has no equal cannot be reduced by an artist's hand into a limited form. And when a Muslim wants to "see" his Lord in his life, he sees Him in the traces of His mercy and power in creation, and in His speech recited in the Qur'an — not in a picture hung on a wall.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Ash-Shura: 11], [Al-An'am: 103], [Maryam: 65]. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of the verse of Ash-Shura 11. - Islam Question & Answer website — answers to "Does Allah resemble His creation?", directed to seekers.

5.4 Merciful and Near: The Other Face That Is Not Often Told

If the common media image presents the God of the Muslims in the image of the angry judge alone, then open the Qur'an yourself and look at the first verse a Muslim reads in every surah and in every prayer: "In the name of Allah, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful" [Al-Fatihah: 1]. For Allah presents Himself to His servants, the first thing He presents them with, by two attributes of doubled mercy: Ar-Rahman (the Vast in mercy, whose mercy has encompassed everything) and Ar-Rahim (the One who bestows His special mercy upon His believing servants). And among His Names that the Qur'an repeats: Al-Ghafoor (the All-Forgiving), At-Tawwab (the Ever-Returning [in forgiveness]), Al-Wadud (the Most Loving), Al-Latif (the Subtly Kind), Al-Halim (the Most Forbearing). And He has said about Himself: "Your Lord has decreed upon Himself mercy" [Al-An'am: 54] — that is, He obligated upon Himself, as a favour and generosity, that He have mercy on His repentant servants.

And among the greatest of what Allah introduced Himself by is a verse Muslims call "Ayat al-Kursi" (the Verse of the Footstool), which they read morning and evening and before sleep: "Allah — there is no deity except Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of [all] existence. Neither drowsiness overtakes Him nor sleep. To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission? He knows what is [presently] before them and what will be after them, and they encompass not a thing of His knowledge except for what He wills. His Kursi extends over the heavens and the earth, and their preservation tires Him not. And He is the Most High, the Most Great" [Al-Baqarah: 255]. Reflect on its picture: Ever-Living, who does not die; Self-Sustaining, who is not heedless of His creation for a moment; He neither drowses nor sleeps; He knows everything; and the preservation of the heavens and the earth does not tire Him. So the Lord who does not sleep on you for a moment is not a distant, forgetful lord; and the nearness here is not nearness of place, but nearness of knowledge, care, and response.

And He is Near, not distant: "And when My servants ask you, [O Muhammad], concerning Me — indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" [Al-Baqarah: 186]. And the Prophet ﷺ said in the sacred hadith (hadith qudsi) that he narrates from his Lord: "I am as My servant thinks I am, and I am with him when he remembers Me" — narrated by al-Bukhari (7405) and Muslim (2675). And in another sacred hadith, Allah addresses His servants: "O My servants, I have forbidden oppression for Myself and have made it forbidden among you, so do not oppress one another" — narrated by Muslim (2577). So the Lord who forbids oppression for Himself and decrees mercy upon Himself is not a heavenly tyrant as He is pictured; rather, He is absolute justice and absolute mercy at one and the same time.

And this mercy does not mean the absence of justice and reckoning; for Ar-Rahim in the Qur'an is Himself Al-Adl, who does not wrong [even] the weight of an atom, and Al-Hakim, who does not leave the oppressor and the oppressed equal. And the meeting of mercy and justice in One God is what makes the relationship with Him a relationship of fear and hope together: a fear that deters from oppression, and a hope that opens the door of repentance however great the sin.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Fatihah: 1], [Al-Baqarah: 255] (Ayat al-Kursi), [Al-An'am: 54], [Al-Baqarah: 186], [An-Nisa: 40]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (7405) and Sahih Muslim (2675) — the hadith "I am as My servant thinks I am"; and Sahih Muslim (2577) — the sacred hadith "O My servants, I have forbidden oppression" — both wordings and the takhrij verified via the Dorar al-Saniyyah hadith encyclopedia (dorar.net). - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the interpretation of the verses of Al-Baqarah 186 and 255.

5.5 Is "Allah" Not a God Specific to Muslims, or a God of the Arabs?

A question often repeated in the West: Is "Allah" another god than the one Christians and Jews worship? The short answer: the word "Allah" is not the name of a new god the Muslims invented; it is the name of Allah in the Arabic language from before Islam, and to this day it remains the word Arab Christians and Arab Jews use in their books and prayers when they speak about Allah the Creator; in the Arabic translations of the Bible, the name of God is translated as "Allah" on every page. Indeed, close words in the sister Semitic languages — such as Hebrew and the Aramaic that Jesus, peace be upon him, spoke — indicate the same One God.

And the Qur'an itself declares that the God of the Muslims is the God of the earlier prophets themselves: "And your god is one God. There is no deity [worthy of worship] except Him, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful" [Al-Baqarah: 163], and it commands Muslims to say to the People of the Book: "And our God and your God is one; and we are Muslims [in submission] to Him" [Al-'Ankabut: 46]. So the difference between Islam and what came before it is not in "which god we worship" in origin, but in the attributes of this One God, how He is worshipped, and what He sent His messengers with — which the units of this path present in turn, for you to look into yourself.

And Muslims add a point they see as important: the name "Allah" in Arabic is a proper name that is neither dualised, nor pluralised, nor feminised, nor masculinised; you cannot say "gods (alihah)" from it, nor a feminine form of it — as if the language itself guards the meaning of the pure Oneness to which the Qur'an calls.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 163], [Al-'Ankabut: 46]. - IslamWeb website — materials on "The name Allah among the Arabs before Islam and after it" and "Allah in the Arabic translations of the Bible." - Islam Question & Answer website — the answer to "Is Allah the God of the Christians and the Jews?", directed to non-Muslims.

5.6 How Does the Human Being Know His Lord? By Reason and Revelation Together

One last question remains in this unit: all this talk about Allah is beautiful, but how does the human being know it is true? Islam answers: by two complementary, not contradictory, means. The first of them is reason, the fitrah, and looking into creation: the Qur'an does not ask the human being to cancel his reason; rather, it rebukes whoever does not use it, and repeatedly calls to looking into the heavens, the earth, and the soul: "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the night and the day are signs for those of understanding" [Al 'Imran: 190]. And the second of them is revelation: reason can point to the fact that this universe has One, Wise Creator, but it cannot on its own know what this Creator wants from His servants, nor what their fate is after death; so the messengers came with revelation, completing what reason began — just as reason points you to the fact that there is a doctor in the city, and the doctor himself points you to the medicine.

5.7 Allah's Most Beautiful Names: A Final Map of Nearness

It remains for you to know how a Muslim gets to know his Lord in his ordinary day, not in books of creed only. For Muslims, Allah has Most Beautiful Names by which He introduces Himself in His Book: Ar-Rahman(the Vast in mercy, whose mercy has encompassed everything), Ar-Rahim(the One who bestows His special mercy upon His believing servants); Al-Malik (the King), Al-Quddus (the Most Holy); Al-Khaliq (the Creator), Al-Bari (the Originator); Al-Ghafoor, Al-Wadud; As-Sami' (the All-Hearing), Al-Basir (the All-Seeing); Al-Hayy (the Ever-Living), Al-Qayyum (the Self-Sustaining). And they are not a cold list to memorise; each name among them is a door of relationship: if you fear, you call upon Al-Qawiyy (the Strong), Al-Aziz (the Mighty); if you sin, you knock at the door of Al-Ghafoor, At-Tawwab; if the ways narrow around you, you ask Ar-Razzaq (the Provider), Al-Fattah (the Opener); and if you are wronged, you are reassured by Al-Adl, Al-Hakam. Notice that Ayat al-Kursi, which you read in section 5.4, gathers the greatest of these names in a single breath: "Allah — there is no deity except Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of [all] existence" — Ever-Living, who does not die; Self-Sustaining, standing over every soul for what it has earned. For Allah, for Muslims, is not a silent "cosmic force" nor a cold philosophical idea, but a Living Lord who knows, hears, draws near, and has mercy — and that is why the relationship with Him could be a relationship of love, supplication, and weeping in the depths of the night, not merely a theoretical acknowledgement of His existence.

And here is a precise difference that summarises the spirit of this whole unit: many seekers arrive with their minds at "this universe must have a Creator," then stop at this dry conclusion as if it were the end of the road. Islam says to you: this conclusion is the door of the road, not its end; for the Creator who brought you into being did not leave you at the limit of rational proof; rather, He introduced Himself to you, name by name and attribute by attribute, and called you to call upon Him by them. And whoever has tried calling upon Allah by His Names — "O Merciful, have mercy on me; O Near, answer me" — knows that Tawhid for Muslims is not a logical issue settled in a debate, but a life lived every day. And these Names have a healing function known to everyone who has tried long anxiety: the one who believes that his provision is in the hand of Ar-Razzaq, not in the hand of his manager alone, sleeps more calmly than the one who hung his heart on human beings like him, who fall ill, die, and change. And the one who believes that justice is in the hand of Al-Hakam, Al-Adl, who does not wrong the weight of an atom, can entrust his grievance — which he was unable to repel in this world — to a court in which no rights are lost, and live the rest of his life free of the bitterness of revenge. That is how Allah's Names work in the believer's soul: they are not information memorised for an exam, but a complete psychological and spiritual equipment for facing fear, injustice, loss, and need — the daily fruit that Tawhid reaps when it moves from the mind to the heart. So if you want to test this whole unit in a single situation before you close it: imagine bad news reaching you tonight — a loss, an illness, or a parting. The human being who lives without a near, merciful God faces the news alone in a silent universe; no one hears him and no one cares about him. But whoever knows Allah by these Names faces the same news knowing that As-Sami' has heard him, that Ar-Rahim is more merciful to him than he is to himself, that Al-Hakim did not decree it in vain, and that Al-Latif is arranging for him from where he does not perceive. The news is one, the night is one, and the whole difference is in who dwells in the heart when the phone rings. And with this, the picture of "the One Allah" with which we began is complete: One, with no partner; resembling none of His creation; Merciful, Near; the human being knows Him by his reason, His revelation, and His Names — and He dwells in his heart before He dwells in his books.
