Unit Eight — The Six Pillars of Faith

1. Opening Hook

A strange man enters a gathering where no one knows him, sits down, asks confident questions, then confirms every answer as if he knew it beforehand. In that short dialogue, Islam summarised its whole heart in six pillars that cannot be seen with the eye — yet they run the lives of two billion people from within. This unit walks in the footsteps of that dialogue, pillar by pillar, and question by question.

2. Executive Summary

In the famous Hadith of Jibril, the Prophet ﷺ defined faith by six pillars: belief in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and divine decree, its good and its bad. By them the believer knows his Lord, and is certain that he is not alone in a universe attended by angels who write and who seek forgiveness, and he is connected to a single chain of revelation from Adam to Muhammad ﷺ, and is certain that death has after it a more just chapter, and is content with Allah's decree in what he does not control while he works hard at what he does control. The fruit of the whole system is a single Qur'anic word: tranquillity. This unit explains each pillar from the angle of its daily effect on a person's life, with deliberate detail for the pillar of decree, because it is the pillar that raises the most questions of our age.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 8 of 12. In the previous unit you saw the five pillars of Islam — outward works upon which a Muslim's life is built; and this unit reveals the corresponding inner building: what dwells in the heart that performs those works? It also stands on its own for anyone who arrives at it from the question "What do Muslims believe?", and its place is after Unit 2 (The One God), upon which its first pillar builds.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: - List the six pillars of faith from their prophetic source (the Hadith of Jibril) and explain the practical difference between "Islam" and "faith" in prophetic usage. - Show the effect of belief in the angels, the books, and the messengers on the believer's feeling and daily behaviour. - Explain the meaning of belief in the Last Day from the angle of modern man's anxiety before death. - Present the equation of decree and choice as Islam balances it, and distinguish it from reading the unseen in the stars and horoscopes. - Picture the Unseen in your ordinary day through documented scenes (angels surrounding the gatherings of remembrance and praying for whoever remains in his place of prayer), and answer the objection "If it is written, why am I held to account?" with its brief answer and the place of its detail in Unit 11.

5. Main Text

5.1 A strange man enters the gathering and asks: a scene that changed how Muslims understand their religion

Picture the scene: the gathering of the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, and the Companions sitting around him. A man enters, extremely white in his garments, extremely black in his hair, no trace of travel visible upon him, and no one among those sitting knows him. He sits down before the Prophet ﷺ until he placed his knees against his knees, and placed his hands on his thighs, and began to ask — and the Companions marvelled: he asks and confirms the answer as if he knew it beforehand!

Picture the scene: the gathering of the Prophet ﷺ in Madinah, and the Companions sitting around him.

He asked him about Islam, and he listed for him the five pillars you read in the previous unit. Then he said: So tell me about faith. He ﷺ said: "That you believe in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, the Last Day, and that you believe in divine decree, its good and its bad." He said: You have spoken the truth. Then he asked him about excellence (ihsan), and he said: "That you worship Allah as if you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you." Then the man departed, and the Prophet ﷺ said to his Companions: "This was Jibril; he came to you to teach you your religion" — narrated by Muslim (8).

Pause for a moment at the beauty of the method: an entire religion summarised in a short dialogue in front of people, with the questions of a "strange" man who resembles you today — you came from outside the gathering, no one knows you, and in your head are questions. This unit walks in the footsteps of that dialogue: six pillars of faith, and for each one we ask Jibril's own question: what does it mean? And why does it matter to me?

Sources: - Sahih Muslim (8) — the famous Hadith of Jibril, from the narration of Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him — verified through the Hadith Encyclopaedia at Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — the Creed Encyclopaedia, the explanation of the Hadith of Jibril.

5.2 Belief in Allah and His angels: believing that you are not alone in this universe

The first of the pillars and their origin: belief in Allah — and you have come to know Him in Unit Two as One, Self-Sufficient, Merciful, Near. Next to it comes belief in the angels: beings of light whom Allah created before mankind, who do not disobey Him in what He commands them and who do what they are commanded, with great tasks: Jibril, the trustee of revelation to the prophets; Mika'il, in charge of rain and provision; angels who bear the Throne; angels who write the deeds of the servants; and the Angel of Death, who takes the souls by his Lord's permission. The Qur'an pictured the two writing angels with an image at which the contemplator stops for a long time: "When the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left. Man does not utter any word except that with him is an observer prepared [to record]" [Qaf: 17–18] — a person utters no word except that with him is a watchful observer, present, writing.

A young person today asks an honest question: why should I believe in a world I cannot see? And the calm answer: because every day you believe in things you cannot see — you do not see gravity, but you see its effect; you do not see your mother's love in a laboratory, but you know it with certainty from its effects. So belief in the unseen in Islam is not a blind leap, but assent to the report of the truthful, trustworthy one about a world whose effects cohere with what we see: an organised, preserved universe, and a living conscience inside you that watches you when no human being sees you. Imagine the effect of this on your day: a Muslim who believes that with him are two angels writing his word and his deed lives with an inner watchfulness that needs no cameras and no traffic laws; and he believes that the angels seek forgiveness for the believers and pray for them, so he feels he is surrounded by a care he cannot see, in a universe that is neither silent nor empty.

Try asking yourself now: if you were certain that every word you say is written and will one day be presented to you, how many words would you have swallowed before they came out this week?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 285], [At-Tahrim: 6], [Qaf: 17–18], [Al-Anbiya: 19–20]. - Abu Ja'far al-Tahawi, Al-Aqidah al-Tahawiyyah — the chapter on belief in the angels (in simplified wording). - Islamweb — the Creed Encyclopaedia, materials on "Belief in the Angels and Its Effect."

Take the questioner's seat: The scene of the previous section is a dialogue: a stranger asks, the Prophet ﷺ answers, and Jibril confirms. Before you go on, write, in your own questions, the first three questions you would have asked had you entered that gathering: a question about Allah, a question about what you do not see, and a question about your destiny. Keep them written down; for at the end of the unit you will return to them and mark, yourself: which of them the six pillars answered, and which remained your open question for your Companion.

5.3 Belief in the books and the messengers: one library, and its messengers are prophets

The third and fourth pillars are twins: that you believe that Allah sent down books as guidance for people — the Torah to Musa, the Psalms to Dawud, the Gospel to Isa, and the Qur'an to Muhammad, peace be upon them — and that He sent human messengers to convey them and to live them. Allah the Exalted said, describing the believers: "The Messenger has believed in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and [so have] the believers. All of them have believed in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers, [saying], 'We make no distinction between any of His messengers'" [Al-Baqarah: 285]. And He said: "O you who have believed, believe in Allah and His Messenger and the Book that He sent down upon His Messenger and the Scripture which He sent down before. And whoever disbelieves in Allah, His angels, His books, His messengers, and the Last Day has certainly gone far astray" [An-Nisa: 136].

And notice the precise difference between "Islam" and "faith" in prophetic usage, as it appeared in Jibril's dialogue: Islam was mostly applied to the outward works (the testimony, prayer, zakah…), and faith was mostly applied to the inward assent of the heart (in Allah, the angels, the books…). So if the two come together in a person, his religion is complete, outwardly and inwardly; and if one of them is mentioned alone, the other enters into it. You are now reading units on the outward and the inward of "Islam" together, because the building is one: pillars that are seen, and pillars that are inhabited.

A small scenario: a young Muslim at a European university is asked by his Christian colleague in amazement: "You believe in our Gospel and our Torah too?" He smiles: "Rather, my faith is not valid until I believe that Allah sent them down upon Musa and Isa, and that I love them and honour them." In this way this pillar turns interfaith dialogue from a struggle over ownership into a brotherhood of a single source, even if the readings of the texts differed after that.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 285], [An-Nisa: 136], [Aal-e-Imran: 84]. - Ibn Abi al-Izz, Sharh al-Aqidah al-Tahawiyyah — for internal review on the difference between Islam and faith (not for direct quotation). - Islamweb — the Creed Encyclopaedia, materials on the six pillars of faith.

5.4 Belief in the Last Day: when the phone rings in the middle of the night

The fifth pillar: that you believe this life has a final chapter — death, resurrection, reckoning, and recompense — and we have spoken to you of its justice in Unit Three. Let us see it here from the angle of today's young person.

Your phone rings at one in the morning: news of the sudden death of a friend of your age, who was laughing with you last week. In moments like these, all the ready-made material answers collapse: "Everything is over" is an answer your heart does not accept as you look at his face in the shroud. Belief in the Last Day tells you something different: not everything is over; your companion has moved from the abode of work to the abode of recompense, and you will meet him, and every person will be paid his work in full, with no decrease. That is why you see the Muslim at the grave of his beloved weeping — for grief is human and legitimate — but he does not collapse the collapse of one who has lost everything forever; he has a promise of meeting, he has a supplication that reaches, and he has a charity that continues to flow for the one who died.

Ask yourself honestly: which of the two conceptions makes you more able to rise tomorrow and continue life with meaning — that death is a final deletion, or that it is a door to a more just and more lasting chapter?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Mulk: 2], [Az-Zalzalah: 7–8], [Ya-Sin: 78–79]. - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at his commentary on verse 2 of Al-Mulk. - Islam Question & Answer (islamqa.info) — answers on "Belief in the Last Day and Its Psychological Effect."

5.5 Belief in divine decree: the hardest pillar, and the deepest in tranquillity

The sixth and last pillar, and the one that raises the most questions: that you believe that Allah knew everything before it came to be, and wrote the measures of creation, and that what He willed was and what He did not will was not, and that He is the Creator of everything. Allah the Exalted said: "Indeed, all things We created with predestination" [Al-Qamar: 49]. And here the mind quickly objects: if everything is decreed, then where is my choice and my responsibility? Am I not compelled, then?

The scholars of Islam answer with a precise balance — try it for yourself: in your daily life you distinguish between what runs over you without choice (the colour of your eyes, your family, an earthquake striking your city) and what you choose with your complete will (to study or neglect, to be truthful or to lie). Decree encompasses both in Allah's knowledge and His creation, but reckoning and responsibility fall only upon the zone of your own choice; for Allah does not hold you to account for your height, nor for an illness that struck you, but for what your hands earned by choice. And that is why the Prophet ﷺ said to the man who asked him about abandoning work in reliance upon decree: "Work, for everyone is facilitated towards what he was created for" — narrated by al-Bukhari (4949) and Muslim (2647); so the Prophet's ﷺ practical answer was: work, do not sit.

And the effect of this pillar on the soul, when it settles, is wondrous: "What reached you was never going to miss you, and what missed you was never going to reach you" — a prophetic rule that he ﷺ said to Ibn Abbas, may Allah be pleased with them both, while he was a boy, teaching him words that would preserve him his whole life (narrated by al-Tirmidhi 2516, who said: a hasan sahih hadith) — so if you believe in it, the panic of "if only I had done such-and-such, such-and-such would have happened" leaves you, and there remains with you one clean energy: taking the means today, and contentment with the results tomorrow. And the Qur'an stated the purpose of this firmness in the soul explicitly: "No disaster strikes upon the earth or among yourselves except that it is in a register before We bring it into being — indeed that, for Allah, is easy — in order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you" [Al-Hadid: 22–23] — "in order that you not despair": decree in the Qur'an is a medicine for grief over what is lost, as much as it is a doctrine about the unseen. And in another verse He described its effect in the believer's heart at calamity: "No disaster strikes except by permission of Allah. And whoever believes in Allah — He will guide his heart" [At-Taghabun: 11] — He guides his heart: He does not prevent him from human grief, but guides him within it so it does not turn into despair. A Muslim like this does not live paralysed in the name of decree; rather, he lives as bravely as can be: he takes lawful risks, bears affliction patiently, and does not collapse at loss, because he knows the story is written by the pen of a Wise, Merciful One, not by the chaos of coincidences.

And stop here at a common crossroads of our age: millions of people open, every morning, the page of "your horoscope today," or pay a fortune-teller and a palm-reader, searching for a map of their future. The motive is understandable and human: fear of the unknown. But notice the essential difference between the two paths: astrology claims that the unseen is written in the movement of stars and planets that can be read and bought, making the person a prisoner of a ready-made map that he awaits and does not make; and belief in decree says exactly the opposite: the unseen belongs to Allah alone — it is neither read nor bought — and your future, you, is in the zone of your choice and your striving, and the means are taken from the earth — from study, work, and consultation — not from the positions of the stars. So the believer in decree does not ask, "What will happen to me?" but asks, "What will I do today?", then hands the result over to the One in whose hand is everything. One path hangs you on a sky to be read, and one path frees you for an earth to be worked — and between them is the difference between real tranquillity and its illusion.

And consider the difference in your ordinary day: whoever opens his morning with his "horoscope" reads his day a passive reading — the horoscope warns him against a decision, so he does not decide, or promises him a happy day, so he acts recklessly — and he becomes a prisoner of fluctuations of which he owns nothing, and whenever a prediction fails he searches for a more accurate astrologer, not for sounder work. But the believer in decree opens his morning with a single working question: "What is in my hand today?" So he studies, works, consults, and trusts, and if the result comes as he loves, he gives thanks, and if it comes contrary to it, he is content and continues, because he knows that a door that closed is not the end of the story but a turn within it. The first path sells you the illusion of knowledge and takes your will from you; the second path admits your ignorance of the unseen to you honestly, and returns your will to you in full, in the only zone you actually own: the zone of your own work. That is why it was no wonder that the nations most believing in decree throughout history were among the most active in building the earth; for whoever is certain that his term and his provision are written no longer fears to take a lawful risk, and whoever doubts everything becomes too cowardly to begin anything.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Qamar: 49], [Al-Hadid: 22–23], [At-Taghabun: 11]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (4949) and Sahih Muslim (2647) — the hadith "Work, for everyone is facilitated towards what he was created for"; and Sunan al-Tirmidhi (2516) — the Prophet's ﷺ instruction to Ibn Abbas, "What reached you was never going to miss you…" — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Islamweb — the Creed Encyclopaedia, materials on "Belief in Decree and Its Relation to Human Choice."

5.6 The Unseen made tangible: scenes from your day that your eye does not see

"The Unseen" might remain a theoretical idea: angels somewhere, and a decree written on some tablet. But the texts make it a daily neighbourhood, in scenes whose effects are tangible even though their very beings are absent from the eye. Three scenes from an ordinary day:

Scene one — a small gathering in a home. A few people studying the Book of Allah together. They see nothing but the book and the faces, yet the Prophet ﷺ told us that Allah has angels who travel about the earth seeking out the people of remembrance; when they find them, they call out to one another: "Come to what you seek," and they surround them with their wings up to the lowest heaven — narrated by Muslim (2700). So the ordinary gathering, in the eyes of its people, is surrounded and called out about in the highest assembly.

Scene two — minutes after the prayer. You prayed, and you stayed in your place of prayer remembering Allah instead of rising to your phone. He ﷺ said: "The angels pray for one of you as long as he remains in the place where he prayed, saying: O Allah, forgive him; O Allah, have mercy on him" — narrated by al-Bukhari (445) and Muslim (649). "They pray for him" means they supplicate for him and seek forgiveness for him; so those silent minutes of yours are not an emptiness, but a time in which supplications you cannot hear are being raised.

Scene three — decree in an ordinary day. A door of promotion you were waiting for has closed, or you missed a train that changed your whole day's plans, and you found your tongue about to say: "If only I had left earlier…" Here the pillar of decree does its daily work. He ﷺ said: "The strong believer is better and more beloved to Allah than the weak believer, and in each there is good. Strive for what benefits you, seek Allah's help, and do not feel helpless. And if something befalls you, do not say: If only I had done such-and-such, such-and-such would have happened; rather say: Allah has decreed, and what He willed He has done — for 'if only' opens the work of Satan" — narrated by Muslim (2664). The hadith teaches you a single sentence, at the first moment of regret, that closes the door of "if only" and opens the door of the next step. That is how decree is practised: a phrase on the tongue at a moment of real loss, and renewed work the next morning.

So the Unseen, for the Muslim, is not darkness behind a curtain, but a gracious neighbourhood: angels who surround, supplicate, and write, and a Lord who decreed with wisdom what you do not control, and left to you what you do control.

Sources: - Sahih Muslim (2700) — the travelling angels; Sahih al-Bukhari (445) and Sahih Muslim (649) — the angels' prayer for the one in his place of prayer; and Sahih Muslim (2664) — the hadith of the strong believer — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net).

5.7 The objection in its strongest form: "If everything is written, why am I held to account?"

And here is the objection as its proponents state it in its strongest form: "You say that Allah knew everything and wrote the decrees, and that what He willed was and what He did not will was not. So if my act tomorrow is known to Him and written before I was born, then I am carrying out a script I did not write; by what justice am I held to account for it?"

This unit's answer — within the limits of its subject, and deliberately in brief — is three ordered layers. First: you do not know what was written, so the writing does not operate in your decision. When you stand tomorrow before a real choice, you will find in your breast nothing but your will and your means; for decree is entirely unseen to you, and that is why the Prophet ﷺ never made the practical answer "wait for what was written," but rather "Work, for everyone is facilitated towards what he was created for." Second: Allah's knowledge of your act does not make your act. That Allah knows your choice from eternity does not mean He compelled you to it; for knowledge, according to Ahl al-Sunnah, uncovers and does not influence, and had you chosen otherwise, Allah would have known your other choice. Third: history tried both extremes, and Islam rejected them together. One group went to excess in decree until it made man compelled and not to be questioned, and another went to excess in choice until it removed the servants' acts from Allah's knowledge and creation; and Ahl al-Sunnah remained upon the middle: a complete decree, a real choice, and reckoning upon the zone of choice alone. Allah the Exalted said, gathering both sides: "And you do not will except that Allah wills" [At-Takwir: 29].

And in complete honesty: this question has finer theological layers whose dedicated place in this path is Unit Eleven, "Common Questions and Objections." Take its practical balance: work as if the matter were in your hand, and be content as if the outcome were in the hand of a Wise, Merciful One — for that is belief in decree as it is lived.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [At-Takwir: 29] — the servant's will within Allah's will. - Al-Tahawi, Al-Aqidah al-Tahawiyyah, and Ibn Abi al-Izz, its commentary — the chapters on decree and will (in simplified wording; and for internal review on the two extremes of compulsion and free-will absolutism).

5.8 Six pillars, one tranquil heart

Now gather the picture: a believer who knows his Lord as One and Merciful, and feels that he is not alone in a universe attended by the angels, and reads a Book that connects him to the chain of all humanity's prophets, and awaits a day on which every wrong will be returned, and is content with Allah's decree in what he does not control, and works hard at what he does control. Are you surprised, after that, that the Qur'an describes the fruit of this whole faith with a single word: tranquillity? "Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured" [Ar-Ra'd: 28].

These are the six pillars of faith: not a list to memorise for an exam, but a map of the Muslim's heart from within.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Ar-Ra'd: 28], [Al-Baqarah: 285]. - Sahih Muslim (8) — the Hadith of Jibril, the framework of the whole unit. - Islamweb — articles on "The Fruits of Faith and the Heart's Tranquillity."

5.9 Ihsan: the third degree with which Jibril concluded his dialogue

Notice that Jibril's dialogue, with which we began, did not stop at Islam and faith; for he asked about a third degree, which he named "ihsan" (excellence), and he ﷺ said: "That you worship Allah as if you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you." This degree is the spirit of all six pillars when they mature: belief in Allah becomes a living presence in the heart, not memorised information; belief in the writing angels becomes a gentle self-watchfulness, not tense fear; belief in the Last Day becomes a calm daily readiness; and belief in decree becomes a working contentment that does not wait to understand everything in order to be at peace. Ihsan is that you live as though there were a permanently open line between you and Allah: you are truthful in your speech when no one hears you but Him, you perfect your work when no manager reviews you, and you restrain your harm when you could harm with no witness. That is why the scholars said that ihsan is the ripe fruit of faith; for the six pillars are the tree's roots and its trunk, and ihsan is its shade and the taste of its fruit in the lives of the people around you.

And a practical question with which this section closes its unit: which situation in your coming week would change if you lived it at the degree of ihsan — as if you see Him? One situation is enough for you to taste the difference between a religion that is known and a religion that is lived. And notice the completeness of the picture Jibril, peace be upon him, drew in his short dialogue: a religion with an outward that is seen (Islam with its five pillars), an inward that is inhabited (faith with its six pillars), and a spirit that watches and perfects (ihsan). Whoever lives by the outward alone, his religion becomes tiring habits; whoever claims the inward without the outward, his religion becomes comfortable slogans; and whoever gathers the three becomes a person complete in religion: his hand works, his heart assents, and his spirit watches Allah in secret and in open. This is the person whom these first eight units together describe, and he is the one you will meet in the next unit as he overflows onto the people around him with character, mercy, and justice. So the six pillars are not the end of the road, then, but the soil in which the rest of the whole tree of the religion grows.

Sources: - Sahih Muslim (8) — the Hadith of Jibril, the definition of ihsan — a reference back to the text of section 5.1. - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — the Creed Encyclopaedia, materials on "Ihsan, Its Degrees and Its Fruits."

Practise it yourself — a record of the unseen in a single day: Section 5.6 built these pillars on scenes from your day. Apply its method to your own day: choose three situations from your yesterday (a call, a decision, a piece of news), and write beside each situation: which of the six pillars was present in it without your seeing it? (An angel writing, a decree proceeding, a Last Day approaching…) Three lines are enough; for belief in the unseen is built, for a Muslim, in this way: seeing what is not seen, present in what is seen.
