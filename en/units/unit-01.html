Unit One — What Is Islam? And Why Does the Human Being Search for the Truth?

WayToNoor — «طريق النور» — "Your way to the Light of Islam — with a companion for your first steps."

Qur'an quotations follow the Saheeh International translation. Hadith quotations follow recognised published English translations, with the same source references (takhrij) as the Arabic edition.

1. Opening Hook

Three o'clock in the morning. The city is asleep, the phone is silent, and everything you run from all day is now standing quietly in front of you: Where did I come from? Why am I here? And what comes after all of this ends? If this question has ever visited you, this unit was written for you — not to press you with a ready-made answer, but to walk with you through a first step, ordered and documented, toward the answer.

2. Executive Summary

The big questions of existence are not a weakness or a passing curiosity; they are the voice of the fitrah — the inner nature upon which the human being was created, in which there is a hunger for meaning that bread alone does not satisfy. And the multiplicity of answers and religions does not cancel the existence of the truth; rather, it calls for comparison and careful examination. "Islam," in its simplest definition, is a voluntary, loving submission to the One God, and it is — in the understanding of Muslims — the religion of all the prophets throughout history, sealed with the preserved message of Muhammad ﷺ. Islam promises a coherent answer, a direct relationship with Allah, and tranquillity of the heart; it does not promise a life without pain or a magic bargain. And this unit does not ask you for a decision; it asks only that you keep looking, honestly.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 1 of 12 in the path "Get to Know Islam," and it is its gateway: it has no prerequisites, and no knowledge of Islam or of Arabic is assumed. If you reached it from the quiz "Where are you in the journey of searching?", from a search engine, or from a friend's referral, you are in the right place. And everything that comes after it in the path is built upon it: in it, Islam is defined, and the method of the whole presentation is set — wisdom, gentleness, and documentation, and an invitation in which there is no pressure.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: - Explain, in your own words, the meaning of the word "Islam" and its linguistic origin. - Show why Islam sees the big questions of existence as a human fitrah, not a weakness. - Explain why the multiplicity of religions and answers is not proof of the absence of the truth. - Distinguish between what Islam actually promises and what it does not promise.

5. Main Text

5.1 Questions That Come in the Quiet of the Night

Perhaps you did not open this page by chance. Behind many visits to pages like this stands an old question, which quietens in the bustle of the day and then returns in the quiet of the night: Where did I come from? Why am I here? And where do I go after this life ends?

These questions are not a sign of weakness, nor evidence of a deficiency in intellect or knowledge. They have accompanied the human being since the earliest ages; the farmer asked them in his field, the philosopher in his library, the doctor after a long day in his hospital, and the mother as she looked at her sleeping child. Indeed, it is striking that the more a person's knowledge of the details of this world increases, the more he feels that the biggest question has not yet been answered: science describes to us how things work, in an astonishing description, but it does not tell us why they existed in the first place, nor what we should do with our one short life, nor what it means that we die.

And so, on this page, you are not asked to leave your mind at the door. Quite the opposite: this path was written for whoever wants to think, to ask, and to weigh the answers calmly. You will find no pressure here to decide anything today, no embarrassing questions about your identity, and nothing but an ordered presentation of what Islam says about itself, from its first sources, for you to look into yourself. For a truth that fears questions is not a truth, and an invitation that respects the human being begins by respecting his mind.

And before we define Islam, let us pause a little at this search itself: why does the human being search for the truth at all? And why does he not content himself with living, eating, working, and sleeping, as the other creatures around him do?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Mulk: 2], on the human being being tested by life and death so that his deed and his purpose may be seen. - Wahiduddin Khan, Islam and the Modern Man — in Arabic: «الإسلام يتحدى: مدخل علمي إلى الإيمان» (Islam Challenges: A Scientific Introduction to Faith), translated by Zafar al-Islam Khan, reviewed and introduced by Abd al-Sabur Shahin — the chapters on the question of the opponents of religion and the need for religion. - IslamWeb website (islamweb.net) — materials introducing Islam and the wisdom of creation.

5.2 Why Do We Search? Because Inside Us Is What Bread Alone Does Not Satisfy

Try reflecting on an ordinary day of your life. You succeed in your work, you eat good food, you laugh with those you love — and yet, at the end of the day, a light feeling of emptiness may steal over you, as if something is still missing. This feeling is not an illness that needs a diagnosis every time; it may simply be the voice of our human nature itself.

For the human being is not a body only. Inside him is another hunger that restaurants do not satisfy, and another thirst that markets do not quench: a hunger for meaning, and a thirst to know that his life is not in vain, that his pain is not without wisdom, and that his death is not the end of the whole story. That is why humanity, in its long history, has never known a society without religion and without questions about the unseen, however much the forms of religiosity differed and varied; the question itself is constant, even if the answers differed.

Islam calls this inner nature the fitrah: that is, the original creation upon which the human being was created, in which there is an innate readiness to know his Creator and to turn to Him. Allah the Exalted said: "So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth. [Adhere to] the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people. No change should there be in the creation of Allah. That is the correct religion, but most of the people do not know" [Ar-Rum: 30]. So religion, in the Islamic conception, is not something strange imposed on the human being from outside him; rather, it is an answer to a call already present inside him — just as thirst is an inner proof that water exists in this world.

And the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ expressed this fitrah in an eloquent image, saying: "No child is born except upon the fitrah, then his parents make him a Jew, or make him a Christian, or make him a Magian" — narrated by al-Bukhari (1358) and Muslim (2658). That is, the child is born upon a sound origin, ready to know his Lord; then comes the role of the environment, upbringing, and society in shaping what he believes later. And this hadith opens an important door to honest thinking: how much of what we believe today did we ourselves choose after looking, and how much of it did we inherit from our surroundings without testing it even once?

The mere fact that you have arrived here, and are reading these lines, means that you have decided to test for yourself. And this is a step not to be taken lightly; many live and die postponing the question to a "later time" that never comes. But you have begun, and an honest beginning is half the road.

And "beginning" here does not mean possessing all the answers at once; it means giving the question itself its due of honest attention. Just as thirst does not disappear by being ignored, so too the questions of meaning do not settle by being repeatedly postponed; they return because they are part of your nature, not something foreign to it. And when you look at them with this calm, the search turns from scattered anxiety into ordered steps: a clear question, an answer that is examined, then a following question that builds on what came before it, calmly, honestly, and with balance.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Ar-Rum: 30]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (1358), Book of Funerals; and Sahih Muslim (2658), Book of Divine Decree — the hadith of the fitrah, its wording and takhrij verified via the Dorar al-Saniyyah hadith encyclopedia (dorar.net). - Abd al-Rahman ibn Nasir al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman fi Tafsir Kalam al-Mannan — at the interpretation of the verse of Ar-Rum 30. - IslamWeb website — articles on "The fitrah and the human being's need for religion."

5.3 If the Question Is One, Why Did the Answers Differ?

A fair question presents itself here: if the need for the truth is innate and universal, why is the world full of different — sometimes even contradictory — religions and directions? And why should I listen to Islam rather than another?

The calm answer begins by acknowledging that difference itself is not proof of the absence of the truth. People differ in medicine, in history, and in economics, and no reasonable person says: therefore there is no truth in these sciences. Difference calls us to comparison and careful examination, not to cancelling the search. And there are understandable reasons for this diversity: some religions had their texts preserved, and some were exposed over the centuries to long oral transmission and repeated editing; some messages were originally directed to particular peoples in particular times; and some answers are of human making themselves: philosophies and ideas that tried to fill the same void without revelation.

Here Islam presents its own reading of this scene — a reading that deserves to be heard fairly: Islam sees that Allah never left humanity for a single day without guidance; rather, He sent to every nation a messenger calling to the same essential message: worship Allah alone, do good, and prepare to meet Allah after death. He the Exalted said: "And We certainly sent into every nation a messenger, [saying], 'Worship Allah and avoid Taghut'" [An-Nahl: 36]. So the origin of the call is one throughout history, even if the languages, the peoples, and the practical details multiplied according to time and place.

But Islam adds a major claim, which we will present in detail in coming units: that this long chain was sealed with a final, universal message whose Book was preserved with a documented preservation, so it remained fit to be the reference against which all the answers are compared. In this first unit, however, it is enough that you agree with us on one thing: the multiplicity of answers is not a sufficient reason to abandon the question. Someone who found many maps of one city in front of him did not tear them all up and sit in his place; rather, he compared them, tested them on the road, and followed the most reliable of them.

The comparison meant here is not a quick gathering of scattered impressions; it is a calm look at the origin of each answer, its evidence, and its consistency with itself and with what you know about yourself. For the answer that deserves to have a whole life built upon it is not measured by its spread alone, nor by its age alone; it is measured by the truthfulness of its source, the clarity of its proof, and its ability to answer the big questions without contradiction or evasion.

That is why this path does not ask you to believe Islam merely because it is one of the options; rather, it invites you to look into its claim itself: What does it say about Allah? What does it say about you? And what is its evidence for what it says? If you find the answer coherent and in keeping with what is in your fitrah and your mind, then you are the owner of the decision. And if questions remain with you, you will find in the eleventh unit of this path a whole door for the difficult questions and the major misconceptions, written without evasion.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nahl: 36], [An-Nisa: 163–165], on the sending of the messengers to the nations. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of the verse of An-Nahl 36. - Wahiduddin Khan, Islam Challenges — on the method of comparison between religion and the materialist philosophies. - Islam Question & Answer website (islamqa.info) — answers to "Why did religions multiply?", directed to seekers.

5.4 What Is Islam, Then? One Word That Carries a Life Project

The word "Islam" is an Arabic word from a root indicating peace, soundness, and voluntary submission. So Islam, in its simplest definition, is: that the human being submits, by his own choice and love, to his One Creator, worshipping Him alone and obeying Him in what He commanded, and finds in this submission his inner peace and his peace with those around him. And "the Muslim" is the one who chose this path. Notice that the definition does not begin with rituals, clothing, or a language; it begins with a relationship: a relationship between the human being and his Lord, built on knowledge, love, and conscious obedience.

Allah the Exalted said: "Indeed, the religion in the sight of Allah is Islam" [Al 'Imran: 19]. And Muslims understand from this verse that "Islam" in this general meaning — submission to Allah alone — is the religion of all the prophets throughout history, from Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus to Muhammad, peace and blessings be upon them all; all of them called their peoples to One God and to one path in its essence. Then "Islam" became a specific name for the final message brought by Muhammad ﷺ, which Muslims see as the last, complete, preserved form of that one, ancient religion. That is why a Muslim does not feel he is embracing a "new" religion that appeared only fourteen centuries ago; rather, he sees himself as an heir to the oldest story in history: the story of the human being with his Lord.

And from this definition branches the complete picture of Islam, which the coming units will take one by one: One God, One, who alone deserves worship (Unit Two); a clear purpose for the creation of the human being, his life, and his death (Unit Three); a chain of prophets with one message (Units Four and Five); a final, preserved Book, the Qur'an (Unit Six); then practical pillars that build the Muslim's daily life (Unit Seven); pillars of faith that build his heart from within (Unit Eight); and ethics that overflow from all of this onto all people (Units Nine and Ten).

And in one word before we move on: Islam is not an ethnic club or a regional culture. Muslims today are nearly two billion people; they speak hundreds of languages, live on every continent, and most of them are not Arabs at all — Arabic is the language of the Qur'an, not the nationality of the Muslim. Whoever entered this religion, from any country, colour, or past, entered as a full brother, not a second-class guest. He the Exalted said: "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you. Indeed, Allah is Knowing and Acquainted" [Al-Hujurat: 13]. So the only criterion of preference with Allah is righteousness (taqwa) and righteous deed — not lineage, colour, or wealth.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al 'Imran: 19], [Al-Hujurat: 13], [Al 'Imran: 85]. - Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari, Sahih al-Bukhari (8); and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, Sahih Muslim (16) — the hadith "Islam is built upon five," for the origin of the practical definition, whose detail will come in Unit Seven. - IslamWeb website — "Introducing Islam," within the encyclopedia of terms and concepts. - Islam Question & Answer website — the answer to "What is Islam?", directed to non-Muslims.

5.5 What Does Islam Promise You? And What Does It Not Promise You?

Before you continue this path, it is your right to know honestly what Islam offers, so that your expectations are not built on rosy images that the religion itself does not approve.

Islam promises you, first, a coherent answer to your big questions: Who created you and why? Who deserves that you turn your heart to Him? And what comes after death? It promises you, second, a direct relationship with Allah, without intermediaries: in Islam, you do not need a person to whom you confess your sins, nor a saint to intercede for you so that you are heard; you call upon your Lord yourself, at any time and in any place, and He is closer to you than you imagine. He the Exalted said: "And when My servants ask you, [O Muhammad], concerning Me — indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" [Al-Baqarah: 186]. And it promises you, third, a tranquillity of a special kind, which the Qur'an described in His saying: "Those who have believed and whose hearts are assured by the remembrance of Allah. Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured" [Ar-Ra'd: 28]; a tranquillity that does not mean the absence of problems, but means that the problems are no longer a meaningless absurdity, nor an end without justice.

In return, Islam does not promise you a life without pain, nor quick riches, nor that your family, health, and financial problems will disappear merely because of your interest in religion. For this world, in the Islamic conception, is a house of testing, not the house of final recompense; and the prophets themselves — the most beloved of creation to Allah — tasted loss, illness, and persecution. Whoever presented religion to you as a magic bargain to solve all the problems of this world has sold you an image the Qur'an does not know. What the Qur'an does know is that the human being connected to his Lord passes through the same pain with a different heart: he knows why he endures, to whom he turns, and what awaits him after patience.

And this difference matters for understanding the promise in its correct form: the tranquillity promised is not a cancelling of pain, but the presence of meaning alongside it; and it is not the absence of questions, but the presence of a reference to return to when they grow intense. In this way expectation remains disciplined from the very start of the road; no disappointment comes from a promise that was never made in the first place, and no enthusiasm is built on an image the religion itself does not approve.

Finally, Islam does not promise you that you will find among all Muslims the complete example of what you read here. Between the texts that are infallible for Muslims and the reality of human beings there is a distance that Muslims themselves acknowledge before anyone else; so if you meet a Muslim who does wrong, he is the one who did wrong, and the fair judgement on Islam remains from its Book, the Sunnah of its Prophet, and his biography — not from the mistakes of its followers — just as medicine is not judged by a negligent doctor, nor the law by a bribed judge. Nevertheless, in this path you will find real models from the biography of the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions, so that you see what this religion looks like when real human beings live it.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Baqarah: 186], [Ar-Ra'd: 28], [Al-Baqarah: 155–157], on testing and patience. - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the interpretation of the verses of Al-Baqarah 186 and Ar-Ra'd 28. - Wahiduddin Khan, Islam Challenges — on the difference between the promises of the true religion and emotional propaganda. - IslamWeb website — articles on "Tranquillity of the heart by the remembrance of Allah."

5.6 An Open Invitation: Take Your Time, Ask, and Compare

We have reached the end of our first unit, and we have not asked anything of you yet — and that is deliberate. For the method we commit to on this whole site is the Qur'an's own method in invitation: "Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best" [An-Nahl: 125]. Wisdom in presentation, good instruction in style, and argument — if the matter requires it — in the best of ways, not the ugliest. So if you are one day convinced, let it be a conviction of mind and heart together; for a faith born of pressure dies at the first test, whereas what is born of honest looking stands firm.

We therefore suggest to you three small practical steps after this unit, with no commitment and no cost: First, write down the questions this page stirred in your mind, however difficult or embarrassing they seem; you will find many of them answered in the following units, and what you do not find, send to us through the questions page. Second, read in the next unit about Allah in Islam: "The One Allah: Who Is Allah in Islam?" — for it is the cornerstone upon which everything after it is built. Third, if you have a translation of the meanings of the Qur'an in your language, open it and read one short surah yourself — and let your judgement on the Book everyone talks about be a direct one, instead of contenting yourself with what its friends and its opponents say about it.

And finally, remember that your search for the truth is not a race against anyone. Some who walked this road reached their conviction in weeks, and some needed years of reading, asking, and comparing — and both groups are respected as long as they are honest in their search. We are here to walk with you in order, documentation, and gentleness, unit by unit, and you alone are the owner of the decision at every step.

We will see you in Unit Two: "The One Allah: Who Is Allah in Islam?"

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nahl: 125], [Al-'Ankabut: 46], on arguing with the People of the Book in a way that is best. - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the Book of Good Manners and the chapter on gentleness, on the origin of kindness to the one being invited. - Islam Question & Answer website — the methodology of its answers for seekers of Islam; and the IslamWeb website — the section on invitation and introducing Islam.

7. Your Question

This unit's question is directed to you personally, not to "people" in general: What is the biggest existential question that returns to you in the quiet of the night? Do not answer here in haste or in beautiful wording; take a minute of silence and name it by its real name in one line.

Reflection task (yours alone; do not send it unless you wish): Write your question on a paper or in your phone, then write beneath it: "Where did this question come to me from? When did it begin? And what have I tried so far to answer it?" Three honest lines are enough. Keep them; you will need them in coming units, and your Companion — if you request an "Ask a Human" session — will begin from them, not from zero.
