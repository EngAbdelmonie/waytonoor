Unit Nine — Character in Islam: Mercy, Justice, and Truthfulness

1. Opening Hook

"Religion is for Allah… and character is something else" — a phrase you hear often. Islam overturns this equation completely: for when its owner ﷺ was asked about his mission, he summarised it as the perfecting of noble character. Worship without character, for it, is a building without fruit. This unit does not settle for slogans: documented stories, situations from your own day, and a single scale: in every living being there is reward.

2. Executive Summary

Character in Islam is a declared goal of the message itself — "I was only sent to perfect noble character" — and the acts of worship are means of building it, not substitutes for it. Its system: a mercy that extends from the human being to every living creature ("In every living being there is reward"), a justice that excludes neither relative nor strongman, even against oneself and one's parents, a truthfulness and trustworthiness that the Prophet ﷺ counted as belonging to the very core of faith, not its embellishments, a dutifulness to parents whose prohibition begins from the word "uff," and a right of the neighbour that Jibril nearly made an heir. And what distinguishes the system: a fixed source that majorities do not change, an inner watchfulness that works when no human being sees you, and a balance among the values, so that mercy does not turn into chaos nor justice into cruelty.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 9 of 12. In Units 7 and 8 you saw the two buildings: the Muslim's outward works and the pillars of his inward heart; and this unit sees the fruit the two buildings together promise: how does the Muslim treat people and creatures? It also stands on its own for anyone who arrives at it from the question "What is the character of Islam?" — the most common question of seekers — and it opens the door naturally to the socially hardest tenth unit: women, family, and society.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: - Explain the place of character in Islam from its founding texts, not from general impressions. - Present the prophetic stories of mercy and justice with their sources, and draw from them the practical rule. - Show how Islam ties truthfulness and trustworthiness to faith itself, and the limits of dutifulness to parents and the neighbour's right. - Answer the question "What does Islam add to universal ethics?" with three specific additions.

5. Main Text

5.1 "I was only sent to perfect noble character": a message summarising itself

If you asked the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ: what is your mission, in a few words? You would find among his answers his saying: "I was only sent to perfect noble character" — narrated by Ahmad (8952) and by al-Bukhari in Al-Adab Al-Mufrad (273), and authenticated by al-Albani. Pause at the phrase: a prophet summarising his entire mission — with its acts of worship, its legislation, and its creed — as the perfecting of noble character. For character in Islam is not the religion's "public relations department"; it is its declared goal; and the acts of worship themselves are means of building it: prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing [Al-Ankabut: 45], zakah purifies the soul of miserliness, fasting trains God-consciousness, and Hajj abolishes the distinctions between people.

Allah the Exalted said: "Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to near relatives and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression. He admonishes you that perhaps you will be reminded" [An-Nahl: 90]. This verse has been recited by Friday preachers in the Muslim world every week for centuries, until it became a moral constitution preserved in the consciousness of the common people before the elite.

Ask yourself before we continue: if the formal acts of worship were removed from any religion and only its character remained, would that suffice to build a human being? Islam answers from the other direction: worship without character is a building without fruit; for whoever prayed, and lied, and cheated, and wronged, has taken from the religion its shell and left its core.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nahl: 90], [Al-Ankabut: 45]. - Musnad Ahmad (8952) and Al-Adab Al-Mufrad by al-Bukhari (273) — the hadith "I was only sent to perfect noble character," authenticated by al-Albani — verified through the Hadith Encyclopaedia at Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the author's introduction on the book's moral purpose.

5.2 Mercy: from a woman punished over a cat, to a man forgiven over a thirsty dog

Hear two short stories that summarise the scale of mercy in this religion. The Prophet ﷺ said: "A woman entered the Fire because of a cat which she had tied up, neither giving it food nor setting it free to eat from the vermin of the earth" — narrated by al-Bukhari (3318) and Muslim (2242). A woman confined a cat until it died of hunger, and this sin — against a dumb animal unable to complain — was the cause of the greatest punishment a person can imagine. And in contrast, he ﷺ told of a man who was seized by severe thirst, found a well, went down and drank, then came out, and there was a dog panting, eating the dirt from thirst, and he said: This one has reached the same thirst that reached me, so he went down and filled his shoe with water, then held it in his mouth until he climbed up and gave the dog to drink, "and Allah thanked him for it and forgave him." The Companions said: O Messenger of Allah, is there really a reward for us in [serving] animals? He said: "In every living being there is reward" — narrated by al-Bukhari (2363) and Muslim (2244).

"In every living being there is reward": every living creature you show mercy to, you have a reward in it. By this scale, the character of mercy in Islam extends from the human being to the animal to the whole environment: the Muslim was forbidden from cutting down a fruit-bearing tree even in war, from spoiling water, and from killing an animal in vain, without need. And Allah the Exalted said of His Prophet: "And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds" [Al-Anbiya: 107] — to the worlds, not to the Muslims alone.

A scenario from your day: you find on your way an exhausted cleaning worker in the midday heat, or an injured cat at the door of your building. For a young man raised on these two stories, the scene does not pass like the clips on his phone; his heart, his pocket, and his hand move. Which upbringing do you want for your children: the one that makes mercy a painful piece of news they watch, or the one that makes it an act whose reward they reap?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Anbiya: 107]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (3318) and Sahih Muslim (2242) — the hadith of the cat; and Sahih al-Bukhari (2363) and Sahih Muslim (2244) — the hadith of giving the dog to drink — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the chapter on gentleness and mercy to creatures.

5.3 Justice, even against the closest people: "If Fatima, the daughter of Muhammad, stole, I would cut off her hand"

In many societies, justice collapses at the first big name. In Islam there is a prophetic sentence that has set this scale right for fourteen centuries: when Usamah ibn Zayd — the beloved of the Prophet ﷺ and the son of his beloved — interceded for a woman of the nobility of her people who had stolen, the Prophet ﷺ became angry and said: "Do you intercede in one of the limits set by Allah?" Then he addressed the people and said: "What destroyed those before you was that when the noble among them stole, they left him, and when the weak among them stole, they carried out the limit on him. By Allah, if Fatima, the daughter of Muhammad, stole, I would cut off her hand" — narrated by al-Bukhari (3475) and Muslim (1688). Fatima: the most beloved of his daughters to him. And the message is clear: a law that exempts the strong is not a law, but a disguised privilege.

And Allah the Exalted said, commanding justice even with those you hate: "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness" [Al-Ma'idah: 8]. Your hatred of a people does not justify your wronging them; for justice, for the Muslim, is worship of Allah, not a service to friends. Indeed, the matter goes further: a testimony of justice even against yourself, your parents, and your closest relatives: "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives" [An-Nisa: 135] — so the scale does not tilt even when the one it tilts towards is the dearest of people to you.

And from the practical biography: Umar ibn al-Khattab, may Allah be pleased with him, wrote to his judge Abu Musa al-Ash'ari a letter that has remained a constitution of justice to this day, commanding him in it to treat the litigants equally in the sitting, the looking, and the speaking, so that no noble man hopes for his partiality and no weak man despairs of his justice. And when a Copt from Egypt complained that the son of its governor, Amr ibn al-As, had struck him with a whip, boasting that he was "the son of the noblest," Umar summoned the governor and his son to Madinah, and gave the Copt the whip to exact retribution himself, and said his famous words: "Since when did you enslave people, though their mothers bore them free?" — a story related by the well-known books of history and biography, such as Ibn Abd al-Hakam in Futuh Misr.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Ma'idah: 8], [An-Nisa: 135], [An-Nahl: 90]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (3475) and Sahih Muslim (1688) — the hadith of the Makhzumi woman and the saying "If Fatima, the daughter of Muhammad, stole…" — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Ibn Abd al-Hakam, Futuh Misr wa Akhbaruha — the story of the Copt and the son of Amr ibn al-As; and Umar's letter to Abu Musa on judiciary, as in the books of Islamic governance.

5.4 Truthfulness and trustworthiness: two small exams every day

Imagine two situations that pass by you almost every month: a cashier in a shop returns to you, by mistake, an amount beyond your due, and does not notice. And an opportunity in an exam or a job interview for a small piece of cheating that "harms no one" and raises your result. For many, these are moments of "social cleverness"; for one raised on the character of Islam, these are the real exams in which character is weighed, not the grand speeches.

The Prophet ﷺ said: "Truthfulness leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise; and a man keeps telling the truth until he becomes a truthful man. And lying leads to wickedness, and wickedness leads to the Fire; and a man keeps telling lies until he is written with Allah as a liar" — narrated by al-Bukhari (6094) and Muslim (2607). And he said, concerning trustworthiness and keeping covenants: "There is no faith for one who has no trustworthiness, and no religion for one who has no covenant" — narrated by Ahmad (12383), authenticated by the verifiers of the Musnad and graded hasan by al-Albani. So lying, cheating, and betraying trust, in Islam, are not "professional mistakes," but cracks in faith itself.

That is why you see, in the lists of the practical traits of the hypocrite against which the Prophet ﷺ warned, four that are purely moral: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; when he is entrusted, he betrays; and when he disputes, he transgresses — as in the agreed-upon hadith from the narration of Abdullah ibn Amr, may Allah be pleased with them both (al-Bukhari 34, Muslim 58). Ask yourself with the honesty of the brave: how many of these four creep into our lives when we justify to ourselves a "white lie" or a promise we do not intend to keep?

Sources: - Sahih al-Bukhari (6094) and Sahih Muslim (2607) — the hadith of truthfulness and lying; and Sahih al-Bukhari (34) and Sahih Muslim (58) — the hadith of the signs of the hypocrite; and Musnad Ahmad (12383) — the hadith of trustworthiness and covenant — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the chapters on truthfulness, keeping covenants, and delivering trusts. - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — the Encyclopaedia of Character.

5.5 Dutifulness to parents and the neighbour's right: the nearest circle first

The character of the great religions is usually measured by their speeches about distant humanity, but Islam begins from the nearest circle: your mother and your father. Allah the Exalted said: "And your Lord has decreed that you not worship except Him, and to parents, good treatment. Whether one or both of them reach old age [while] with you, say not to them [so much as], 'uff,' and do not repel them but speak to them a noble word" [Al-Isra: 23]. "Uff": the smallest word of irritation the tongue knows, forbidden in the face of one's parents' old age. Indeed, the Qur'an commands that you lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy, and that you pray for them as they raised you when you were small: "And lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy and say, 'My Lord, have mercy upon them as they brought me up [when I was] small'" [Al-Isra: 24] — for dutifulness, for it, is not a service performed, but a mercy recalled and a memory of kindness that never ends. And a man came to the Prophet ﷺ asking him: Who is most deserving of my good companionship? He said: "Your mother." He said: Then who? He said: "Your mother." He said: Then who? He said: "Your mother." He said: Then who? He said: "Then your father" — narrated by al-Bukhari (5971) and Muslim (2548). Three quarters of dutifulness belong to the mother alone.

Then the circle widens to the neighbour, until the Prophet ﷺ said: "Jibril kept recommending the neighbour to me until I thought he would make him an heir" — narrated by al-Bukhari (6015) and Muslim (2625); that is, would make him an inheritor like the relatives, from the abundance of the recommendation concerning him. And the neighbour in Islam is not required to be a Muslim; for good neighbourliness is a right of shared residence itself.

A story from your own home: how many times this month did you enter your building and pass the door of a sick or elderly neighbour without the thought of knocking crossing your mind? Islam makes that knock an act of worship, and makes ignoring it a deficiency in religion, not only in taste.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Isra: 23–24], [Luqman: 14–15]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (5971) and Sahih Muslim (2548) — the hadith "Your mother, three times"; and Sahih al-Bukhari (6015) and Sahih Muslim (2625) — the hadith of the recommendation of the neighbour — verified through Dorar Al-Sunnah (dorar.net). - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the chapters on dutifulness to parents and the neighbour's right.

5.6 Shared values and an Islamic distinctiveness: why "universal ethics" alone does not suffice

You may say: truthfulness, justice, and mercy are values among all nations, so what does Islam add? A fair question, and its answer lies in three additions. The first is the source: for character, for the Muslim, is not a changing social agreement nor a personal taste, but a command from Allah that stands firm, so majorities do not alter it and interests do not drop it. The second is the watcher: for the Muslim does good even if no human being sees him and no camera deters him, because his watcher is Allah — and that is the meaning of "ihsan" in the Hadith of Jibril: that you worship Allah as if you see Him. And the third is comprehensiveness: for the system is one and coherent: mercy without justice turns into chaos, justice without mercy turns into cruelty, and truthfulness without wisdom harms; and Islam regulates them all with a single scale.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Hujurat: 13], [Al-Ma'idah: 8]. - Sahih Muslim (8) — the Hadith of Jibril, the definition of ihsan. - Islamweb — the Encyclopaedia of Character, materials on "The Sourcedness of Character in Islam and Its Characteristics."

5.7 When character stumbles: what does a Muslim do when he fails his own scale?

A final question without which the picture of character in Islam is not complete: this system has a high ceiling — constant truthfulness, justice even against oneself, mercy to every living being — so what of the ordinary person who sometimes lies, and gets angry, and wrongs others, then regrets it? Does the system expel him from the religion at his first slip? The answer is an original part of this religion's beauty: the door of repentance is open, not closed in anyone's face, so long as the sun has not risen from its place of setting, and Allah rejoices at His servant's repentance with a joy the Prophet ﷺ described with an unforgettable image in the famous hadiths of repentance. But repentance, in practical character, is not a feeling of regret only; it has two parts: a part between you and Allah — regret, desisting, and resolve not to return — and a part between you and people, without which the first is not complete: that you return the money you took without right to its owner, that you apologise to the one you wronged with a word, and that you repair what you corrupted with your hand as far as you are able. For a wrong done to a human being is not erased by tears in a closed room; it is erased by returning rights and seeking the pardon of their owners, and then Allah's pardon comes after that, vast.

This meaning has a deep social effect: a society whose members believe that the door of return is open and that apologising is an act of worship is a society in which disputes are repaired before they swell, and in which the one who errs confesses before he is exposed, because confession, for it, is a path of salvation, not an eternal stigma. Compare that with the culture of "never apologise," which turns every small mistake into an endless battle of dignity. For character in Islam is not a club for the perfect from which the stumblers are expelled; it is a long road with a door of entry and a door of return, and the one walking it falls and rises — and what matters, with Allah and with people, is that he rises in the right direction and returns what he owes along the way.

And here this unit meets its practical challenge, which you began in the field "Your Question": one character trait for seven days. For the great moral system you have read — mercy, justice, truthfulness, dutifulness — does not enter your life through a moving speech, but through a small, repeated act: that you return the extra amount once, and trustworthiness becomes for you a habit, not a painful decision every time; that you tell the truth in a costly situation once, and discover that the sky did not fall and that your respect for yourself rose; that you knock on a neighbour's door once, and doors of relationships you had not reckoned on open to you. That is how character is truly built: small, truthful decisions accumulating until they become a nature, exactly as the habits you want to change were built in the first place — a small act upon a small act. So do not wait for a "comprehensive transformation" that begins some day; begin with one trait this week, and let the whole system enter through its door. And you will discover at the end of your first week of the challenge a small, great truth: that the hardest thing in character is not understanding it — for you understood it in pages — but moving it from the zone of "I know" to the zone of "I do"; and that the distance between the two zones is crossed only by short, truthful repetition. And for this very reason this whole project was designed upon performed practice, not upon reading alone: for every unit in it ends with a step, and every level of its journey with a task that is done and written down. So you are not reading about Islam here a tourist's reading; you are training in pieces of it in safety, so that you arrive at your great decision — if you arrive at it — having tried something of its reality with your own hand and heart, not only heard about it. So if your first week of challenge ends, do not ask yourself, "Have I become a person of complete character?" but ask it a truer and more useful question: "Has trustworthiness become easier for me today than it was seven days ago?" If the answer is yes — even by a little — then you have understood how character is truly built in this religion: one small, truthful brick, upon a brick, upon a brick, until the building stands.

Sources: - Sahih al-Bukhari (6094) and Sahih Muslim (2607) — the hadith of truthfulness and lying, a reference back to the text of section 5.4 on the chapter of returning to truthfulness. - Al-Nawawi, Riyad as-Salihin — the chapters on repentance and returning wrongs. - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — the Encyclopaedia of Character, materials on "Repentance from Wrongs and Returning Rights."

7. Your Question

Which value of this unit's values does your city or your environment lack most: mercy, justice, truthfulness, dutifulness to parents, the neighbour's right? And why did you choose it? One-character challenge — 7 days (the unit's interactive element): Choose one trait only from the unit — returning money that is not yours by right, a costly word of truth, knocking on a neighbour's door, feeding an animal, a call to a father or a mother — and commit to it as a daily act for seven days. Every day, record a single line: what did you do, and what did you feel? And you will sleep after the first day a different person — see how you sleep.

10. Companion Card and Published Times

  • Ready discussion points: (1) Which value did the learner find lacking in his environment (the answer to "Your Question")? — This is the entry point of his personal values. (2) Which story shook him most: the cat, the dog, or Fatima and justice? And why? (3) The challenge: which trait did he choose? And how was his first day?
  • Suggested follow-up questions: How did the days of the challenge pass? Did you face the trustworthiness situation (the extra amount) or something like it? Did the stories change your view of the very concept of "religiosity"?
  • Warning signs and escalation: Confusion between the character of the texts and the real behaviour of some Muslims ← the two concepts are calmly separated (the text is a scale by which reality is judged, not the reverse), and his questions are documented for Unit 11; a repeatedly stumbling challenge ← the size of the daily act is reduced and the challenge is not cancelled (a small, constant act is better than interrupted enthusiasm); the challenge's stories are not used as posts except with explicit, revocable permission.
  • The piece's place in the weekly work plan: Following up the challenge with a short message daily (7 days), and at the weekly meeting the seven lines of the record are read and completion is celebrated before moving to Unit 10.
  • Published times: Reading the text about 10 minutes · watching the video 5–7 minutes · listening to the full reading about 13 minutes (labelled production estimates, as in the Arabic edition).
