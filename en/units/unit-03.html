Unit Three — Why Did Allah Create Us? The Meaning of Life, Death, and Destiny

WayToNoor — «طريق النور» — "Your way to the Light of Islam — with a companion for your first steps."

Qur'an quotations follow the Saheeh International translation. Hadith quotations follow recognised published English translations, with the same source references (takhrij) as the Arabic edition.

1. Opening Hook

In a hospital corridor, or on an aeroplane seat on a long night, or before the grave of a dear one… the human being stands alone with a question for which the answers of the daytime are no use: Why? Why am I here in the first place? And why do those we love die? If life had no meaning, this question would not hurt you in the first place — for pain itself may be the first witness that you were made for a meaning. This unit walks with you in this question slowly and honestly, without ready-made preaching.

2. Executive Summary

Allah created the human being for a noble purpose: that he worship Him by his choice and his love, with a worship that encompasses his whole life, not rituals isolated from it. And He is free of need of our worship; the command to it is for our benefit, nourishment for our souls, and liberation for us from servitude to anything besides Him. And this world, for Him, is a temporary house of testing, not a house of residence; pain and death in it are real and acknowledged, but they are not the end of the story: after them is another Day in which complete justice is established, and not an atom's weight of good or evil is lost. And whoever knows this purpose, his view of his time, his work, and his loss changes from today — not from the Hereafter only.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 3 of 12. It assumes you have passed through Unit Two, "The One Allah," and so have come to know who Allah is in Islam: One, Merciful, Wise, free of need of His creation. If you arrived here directly, the unit stands on its own, but your return to the second will deepen its foundation. And its place here is deliberate: after the question "Who is Allah?" comes inevitably the question "Then why did He create us?" — and from its answer the story of the prophets opens in Unit Four.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to: - Explain the purpose of the human being's creation in Islam, and the meaning of "worship" in its wide, comprehensive sense. - Answer the objection "If Allah is free of need of us, why did He command us?" with Islam's own logic. - Describe the place of pain and death in the Islamic conception: a temporary test, not a final absurdity. - Show how faith in the Last Day gives justice its meaning, and how the answer to purpose changes the human being's daily life. - Face the objection of "the problem of evil" in its strongest formulation, and distinguish in its answer between evil made by human beings, pain from the nature of this perishing world, and the limits of the human being's knowledge. - Read the story of Yusuf, peace be upon him, as a reading of meaning: how successive trials are transformed, in the scale of faith, into steps — not into proofs of absurdity.

5. Main Text

5.1 The Question of Purpose: Why Are We Here in the First Place?

In the previous unit we came to know who Allah is in Islam. Immediately after it comes a question no less pressing: if Allah is free of need, complete, in need of nothing, why did He create us? And what does He want from this short life, full of work, weariness, and loss?

In the previous unit we came to know who Allah is in Islam.

The Qur'an answers this question with one explicit verse that every Muslim memorises: "And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me" [Adh-Dhariyat: 56]. The word "worship" may be misunderstood if it is reduced to limited rituals; but in the understanding of Islam's scholars, worship is a comprehensive name for everything Allah loves and is pleased with, of outward and inward words and deeds: prayer is worship, the merchant's honesty is worship, your dutifulness to your mother is worship, your excellence in your work is worship, and your withholding harm from people is worship. So the purpose of creation is not that the human being spend his life isolated in a prayer niche, but that he live his whole life — home, market, office, and road — connected to his Lord, a servant of His by his choice and his love, neither coerced nor mechanical.

And here is an essential difference: the angels worship Allah without choice and without the possibility of disobedience, whereas the human being was created with a choice, able to obey and to disobey, to believe and to disbelieve. So when the human being chooses obedience with his full will, he has realised something that exists nowhere else in the universe besides him: free worship from a being who was capable of its opposite. That is why the human being's life, for Allah, was too precious to be in vain, and why his reckoning after death is justice in which there is no wrong. And that is why the Qur'an describes this world itself as the arena of this free choice: "Indeed, We have made that which is on the earth adornment for it that We may test them [as to] which of them is best in deed" [Al-Kahf: 7] — for the adornment of the earth is not a purpose in itself; rather, it is an arena in which it is seen: which of the people is best in deed?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Adh-Dhariyat: 56], [Al-Kahf: 7]. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of the verse of Adh-Dhariyat 56, and the comprehensive meaning of worship among the Salaf. - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the interpretation of the verse of Adh-Dhariyat 56. - Islam Question & Answer website (islamqa.info) — the answer to "What is the wisdom behind creating creation?"

5.2 If Allah Is Free of Need of Us, Why Did He Command Us to Worship Him?

A clever objection many people make: if Allah does not need our worship — and He is indeed so in the creed of Muslims, for our obedience adds nothing to His dominion and our disobedience takes nothing from it — why did He command us to it in the first place? Is the command not in vain, then?

Islam answers: the command is for our benefit, not for His. For worship, in its reality, is the nourishment of the soul and its medicine, like food for the body and exercise for the muscles; Allah is free of need of our eating, but we die if we do not eat. So when Allah commands the human being to remember Him, pray to Him, and call upon Him, He commands him to what sets his soul right, settles his life, and corrects his moral compass. He the Exalted said in the sacred hadith: "O My servants, you will not attain harming Me so as to harm Me, and you will not attain benefiting Me so as to benefit Me" — narrated by Muslim (2577). So the equation is clear: the benefit is all returning to the servant, and the freedom from need is all Allah's. And the Qur'an declared this in the context of the stories of the prophets themselves: "And Moses said, 'If you should disbelieve, you and whoever is on the earth entirely — indeed, Allah is Free of need and Praiseworthy'" [Ibrahim: 8] — for even the disbelief of the whole earth takes nothing from His freedom from need, and the obedience of the whole earth adds nothing to Him; the only beneficiary of worship is the worshipper himself.

And consider the wisdom from another angle: the human being is a creature who necessarily worships something; whoever does not worship Allah worships his desire, his money, his appetites, or people's pleasure with him, and becomes a captive of small gods that neither satisfy nor have mercy. So the command to worship Allah alone came as a liberation of the human being from every other servitude, not as a new enslavement of him. It is the only servitude that makes its owner free of everything besides it.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Adh-Dhariyat: 57], [Ibrahim: 8]. - Sahih Muslim (2577) — the sacred hadith "O My servants" — verification via the Dorar al-Saniyyah hadith encyclopedia (dorar.net). - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the interpretation of the verse of Ibrahim 8. - IslamWeb website (islamweb.net) — articles on "The servants' need for worship and Allah's freedom from need of them."

Dialogue — the question of freedom from need: A seeker says to his companion: "If Allah is free of need of me and of my worship, why does He command me to it? Is the command not a sign of need?" His companion answers him with a question of the same kind as his: "And does the doctor command his patient to take the medicine for the doctor's need, or for the patient's need?" Pause at your own answer before reading the next section: write in one line: what is the difference, for you, between a command whose commander benefits from it, and a command whose commanded one benefits from it? That line of yours is the key with which the rest of the unit will be opened.

5.3 Then Why Pain and Death? Life Is a House of Testing, Not a House of Residence

There remains the hardest and most honest question: if the purpose is this beautiful, why is the road full of pain, illness, earthquakes, and the loss of loved ones? And why do we die in the first place?

Islam says, with a frankness that much dawah literature cannot say: this world is not Paradise, and Allah never promised anyone that it would be so. This world, in the Qur'anic conception, is a temporary house of testing, in which the human being's will, honesty, patience, and choices are measured, and then he moves to a permanent house in which is the complete recompense. He the Exalted said: "[He] who created death and life to test you [as to] which of you is best in deed — and He is the Exalted in Might, the Forgiving" [Al-Mulk: 2]. And He said: "And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient" [Al-Baqarah: 155]. So pain exists, is acknowledged, and the one patient over it is promised a reward that is not lost — it is neither denied, nor beautified, nor cast upon the shoulders of a "mysterious fate" without meaning.

And notice the precision of the Qur'anic picture here: Allah does not say that pain is pure evil, nor does He say it is an illusion; rather, He makes it part of the nature of the house of testing itself, and makes the human being's stance toward it — patience, seeking reward, and righteous deed despite the ache — the real material of the test. For pain, for a Muslim, is not proof of Allah's absence, just as it is not an automatic punishment for a particular sin; it is the nature of the stage, and the stage is temporary, with certainty.

As for death, it is not, in Islam, annihilation and nothingness, but a transition: a door through which the soul crosses from the house of deed to the house of recompense. That is why a Muslim does not look at the shortness of this world with the look of the despairing, but with the look of the traveller who knows that his stay in the hotel is temporary however long it lasts; he neither builds in it the building of a resident, nor despairs of the narrowness of its room, because he knows that his real homeland is ahead, not behind. And from here a Muslim understands why this conception lightened great calamities for millions of people throughout history: a calamity that has a meaning, a reward, and an end is not like an absurd calamity that means nothing and ends in nothing.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Mulk: 2], [Al-Baqarah: 155–157], [Al-Mu'minun: 115]. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of the verses of Al-Mulk 2 and Al-Baqarah 155. - Wahiduddin Khan, Islam Challenges — the chapters on purpose, resurrection, and the meaning of testing. - Islam Question & Answer website — answers to "Why do evils and calamities exist?", for seekers.

5.4 What Comes After Death: The Last Day, and a Justice in Which Not an Atom's Weight Is Lost

If this world is a test, the test must have a result, otherwise it would be in vain. And it is a question the Qur'an itself poses to whoever thought otherwise: "Then did you think that We created you uselessly and that to Us you would not be returned?" [Al-Mu'minun: 115]. And here comes a great pillar among the pillars of faith in Islam: faith in the Last Day, a day on which people will stand before the Lord of the worlds, deeds will be weighed, the oppressed will be given redress from the oppressor, and every human being will be recompensed for what he earned. He the Exalted said: "Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your [full] compensation on the Day of Resurrection" [Al 'Imran: 185] — for the compensations are not paid in full here; they are paid in full there. And He said: "So whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it. And whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" [Az-Zalzalah: 7–8]. And He said: "And We place the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, so no soul will be treated unjustly at all. And if there is [even] the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth. And sufficient are We as accountant" [Al-Anbiya: 47].

Reflect on what this means in your daily life before anything else: a tyrant who died before anyone tried him, a wronged child who never knew justice in this world, an unknown doer of good whom no human thanked, and an oppressor who consumed another's lifetime and then slept content — all of these, in the scales of this world alone, are incomplete stories without a just ending. So the Last Day came to be the ending: no good is lost, however small; no evil escapes, however hidden; and the human being's story does not end at his grave. That is why faith in the Hereafter, for Muslims, is not cold information about the unseen, but a daily moral engine: it deters the oppressor when no one sees him, gives patience to the oppressed when no one does him justice, and preserves a reward for the doer of good even if all people forget him.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Mu'minun: 115], [Al 'Imran: 185], [Az-Zalzalah: 7–8], [Al-Anbiya: 47], [Al-Kahf: 49]. - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the interpretation of the verse of Al-Anbiya 47. - IslamWeb website — the encyclopedia of creed, materials on faith in the Last Day and its effect on the human being's life.

5.5 How Does This Answer Change Your Life Today, Not Your Hereafter Only?

You may say: all of this is talk about the unseen and destiny, so what is its effect on my ordinary day? The answer is that the conception of purpose changes everything practically, whether you feel it or not. And the Qur'an summarised this effect in the shortest surah read every day: "By time, Indeed, mankind is in loss, Except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience" [Al-'Asr: 1–3] — the human being is in realised loss merely by the passing of time, except for whoever filled his time with faith, righteous deed, truth, and patience. For time itself is the arena of the test and its capital.

Whoever knows that he was created for a purpose, his time becomes a capital not to be wasted on what has no meaning; the hour that passes does not return, and it is part of the test itself. And whoever knows that his deed is reckoned against him to the atom's weight, his trustworthiness in his job, his honesty in his selling, and his justice with his opponent become worship, not mere "professionalism." And whoever knows that provision and death are in Allah's hand is liberated from the humiliation of flattering human beings and from the panic of loss; he strives with all the means of this world while his heart is attached to the One in whose hand are the results. And whoever knows that beyond death is a life, his loss of his loved ones is no longer the end of the story, but a temporary parting on a long journey, so he is patient, seeks reward, and completes the road.

That is why Islam's answer about purpose was not an escape from life to death, but a return to life in its complete meaning: a life with a known beginning, a noble purpose, a just end, and a clear road.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-'Asr: 1–3], [Al-Mu'minun: 115], [Al 'Imran: 185]. - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Azim — at the interpretation of Surah Al-'Asr. - Wahiduddin Khan, Islam Challenges — on the effect of the creed of the Hereafter in building the human being. - IslamWeb website — articles on "The effect of faith in the Last Day on a Muslim's life."

5.6 When You Do Not Understand the Wisdom: The Art of Contentment That the Believer Learns

You will say — and it is your right to say: "I understood that this world is a house of testing, but my heart still asks at every calamity: Why me? And why now?" Islam does not forbid this question or suppress it; the prophets themselves passed through moments of pain which they expressed to their Lord as a complaint, not an objection. But it teaches you a psychological and spiritual art called contentment (rida): that you distinguish between what you can change — so you change it with all your strength, taking the means — and what has occurred and passed and no one can bring back, so you submit in it to Allah's knowledge and wisdom, even if its details are hidden from you. Contentment is not lazy surrender or emotional numbness; the contented believer weeps at the grave and grieves at loss, but he does not turn his grief into resentment against destiny that eats the rest of his life. It is the difference between a wound that heals and a wound that bleeds every day because its owner reopens it with the question "if only," which has no answer in this world.

And this art has a practical fruit noticed by everyone who has lived around settled believers: an astonishing ability to rise after disasters. His trade collapsed, so he began anew without bitterness; he lost a dear one, so he wept and then returned to his life, his work, and his worship with a calm heart. The secret is not hardness of heart or coldness of nerves; the secret is that he sees his story as part of a greater story written by a Wise, Merciful pen, and that the chapters of pain in this story are not its last chapters. When this vision settles, your question itself changes: from "Why did this happen to me?" to "What does Allah want me to learn and do now?" — and the second question is a question a human being can live by and work with, whereas the first is an open door onto an endless whirlpool.

And contentment has a practical sign by which you can test yourself before you test your creed: look at your relationship with what has passed you by in your life — a job you did not get, a relationship that was not completed, an opportunity lost in a single moment. The person stuck in resentment replays these files every night as if they happened yesterday, and so pays their price twice: once when they occurred, and a thousand times when he recalls them. But whoever has trained in contentment looks at them with the look of one who closed a file and handed it to a wise archive: he remembers the lesson and leaves the pain. Not because his memory is weak, but because his heart is occupied with what is in his hands of a purpose that deserves his energy. And here exactly this unit's question meets the question of purpose with which we began: whoever knows why he was created knows how to carry what he meets on the road; and whoever is ignorant of the purpose, every stone on the road becomes an existential riddle threatening the whole meaning. Contentment, then, is not a lesson in patience only; it is a direct fruit of the answer to purpose when it settles in the heart with its complete settling.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Al-Kahf: 7], [Ibrahim: 8] — a reference to the texts of the previous sections on the meaning of testing and Allah's freedom from need. - IslamWeb website (islamweb.net) — materials on "Contentment with decree and destiny and its psychological fruits."

5.7 Your Purpose, You: A Final Question Before You Fold the Unit

Let us return this whole unit to one personal heading: you. For the questions of purpose are not studied as geography is studied; their value is in changing the way you wake up tomorrow morning. If this life has a Creator free of need of you, who created you for a noble purpose — His worship in its wide meaning: building the earth, doing good to creation, and knowing Him, glory be to Him — and this world is a short house of testing followed by a just house of recompense, then three things change in your day immediately: The first is that your ordinary work itself — your job, your study, your dutifulness to your family — becomes part of your purpose, not an interval apart from it; you do not live a "religious life" on the weekend and a "real life" the rest of the days, but one life of connected meaning. The second is that your measure of success changes: the richest of people or the most famous of them is no longer necessarily "the most successful"; rather, it is whoever realised the purpose of his existence — and by this measure, a simple worker with a reassured heart may be more successful than an anxious billionaire who did not know why he lives. And the third is that death itself changes its place on your psychological map: it is no longer a black wall at which the story ends, so that you fear it with the fear of nothingness; rather, it is a door to the most just and most lasting chapter of your story — you fear what is after it with the fear of the examinee before the result, a fear followed by work and preparation, not the fear of absurdity, which is followed only by escape into pleasure or despair.

And perhaps you noticed that this unit did not answer the question of purpose with an abstract philosophy, but with a connected chain: a Creator free of need and Wise; a human being honoured and given choice; a short world of testing; and a Hereafter of just recompense. Ask yourself now with the honesty of those who try: if you lived one single week with this vision in full — waking for a purpose, working for a purpose, and sleeping knowing that your story does not end at the grave — would your day remain as it is? Try it as an honest experiment, not a theory: one week in which you wake to the question "What is my purpose today?" instead of the question "What is my mood today?", deal with your work, your study, and your family as chapters of a story that has meaning and direction, and close your day with a short reckoning: What did I offer my purpose today? Seven days only are enough for you to feel the difference between a life that is consumed and a life that is built; and if you find the difference real — and you will find it — know that you have not yet touched anything but the edge of the answer Islam presents in full, and that the rest of the path will take you to its depth: from purpose to the Owner of purpose Himself, and from theoretical meaning to a life lived by it every day. For a purpose that does not turn into a daily schedule remains a beautiful idea in a book; but when it becomes a reason for you to wake, a criterion for you to choose your work, your words, your money, and your time, and a scale by which you reckon yourself before sleep, it has become a life. And this is the bridge on which the rest of the units of this path cross with you: from "Why was I created?" to "How do I live what I was created for?" — question after question, and rung after rung on the ladder that leads to the Light.

5.8 The Objection in Its Strongest Form: If Allah Is Merciful and Wise, Why Do Evil and Pain Exist in the First Place?

In its strongest form, as the critics of religion formulate it: "You say that Allah is complete in power, knowledge, and mercy; so He is either incapable, or not merciful, or not knowing — and in every case the perfection collapses. And if He were merciful, He would have created a world without children's cancer, earthquakes, or tyrants." This is "the problem of evil" — the strongest objection to faith — and Islam's answer to it is in layers:

The first layer: Much evil is made by the hands of human beings, and it is the price of real freedom. Wars, injustice, and corruption are human decisions, not heavenly acts; for the human being is given choice, not made mechanical, and real freedom does not exist in a version of "choose good only." He the Exalted said: "Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned" [Ar-Rum: 41]; and the Day of Reckoning is the place of calling every oppressor to account for his choice.

The second layer: Pain from the nature of a perishing house that works by fixed laws. Earthquakes and illnesses are not "decisions" against particular persons; they are the results of a material world of regular laws — the plates that make the mountains are the very ones that tremble, and the cells that grow are the very ones that may go wrong — and a world without fixed laws has neither life in it nor responsibility. He the Exalted said: "We have certainly created man into hardship" [Al-Balad: 4].

The third layer: The limits of our knowledge of the wisdom. Some pain has its wisdom appear after years, and some does not appear. The Qur'an presents this limit with the story of Musa with the righteous servant (Surah Al-Kahf): acts that appear to be pure evil — scuttling the ship of poor people, killing a boy, and setting up a wall for a miserly people — then it is disclosed that behind them is a wisdom not seen from the angle of the first spectator. So our judgement "there is no wisdom here" may be a judgement from inside the scene, not from outside it: "Perhaps you dislike a thing and it is good for you; and perhaps you love a thing and it is bad for you. And Allah Knows, while you know not" [Al-Baqarah: 216].

The fourth layer: What remains after the layers. With complete honesty: Islam does not claim to explain the cause of a specific calamity for a specific person; for that is not among what human beings were given. What Islam offers instead of the complete explanation is three things: a meaning (a house of testing, not absurdity), a promise (complete justice on the Day of Recompense), and companionship (mercy, reward, and a supplication that is heard).

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Ar-Rum: 41], [Al-Balad: 4], [Al-Baqarah: 216], [Al-Kahf: 60–82]. - Al-Sa'di, Taysir al-Karim al-Rahman — at the two verses; and the Islam Question & Answer website — answers on "the problem of evil"; and Elshinawy (Yaqeen, 2018) — the framework of the layers.

5.9 A Story of Meaning Worked Through, Not Merely Told: Yusuf — From the Well to Empowerment

The stories in the Qur'an are not told for enjoyment; they are told as a lens through which you see your story. And the closest of them to the question of this unit is the story of Yusuf, peace be upon him, "the best of stories": a child snatched from his father by his brothers' envy and cast into a well, then sold as a foreign slave, then accused wrongfully and imprisoned for years while innocent. If the story had stopped at any station, it would have appeared to be pure injustice. But the complete thread: the prison itself led him to the interpretation of the king's dream, and the interpretation to the treasuries of Egypt, and the treasuries saved the whole region — and his brothers themselves — from a famine of seven years. And at the moment of empowerment he said a word that summarises the philosophy of the story: "Indeed, he who fears Allah and is patient, then indeed, Allah does not allow to be lost the reward of those who do good" [Yusuf: 90].

And on the other bank stands Ya'qub, peace be upon him, who lost his son for decades until his eyes whitened from grief, and he did not say that life is absurd; he said: "He said, 'I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know'" [Yusuf: 86]. Notice: the complaint was not forbidden; it was directed to Allah, not against Him. So the story does not say "do not suffer"; it says: pain is a chapter, and judging the book from a single chapter is an error of reading before it is an error of faith. And not every story ends in this world with Yusuf's ending — some threads are not completed except on the Last Day (5.4) — but it grants your heart a certainty that the trial is not necessarily the last word.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: Surah Yusuf [12] — and especially [Yusuf: 86, 90]. - Ibn Kathir; and Al-Sa'di — at the interpretation of Surah Yusuf.

5.10 What Islam Does Not Promise You: Honesty Before You Close the Unit

Let us close the main text with its most honest part: three things Islam does not promise you, so that you do not build your faith on an illusion that breaks at the first calamity. It does not promise you that pain will disappear if you believe; for the prophets themselves tasted loss, prison, and illness. Nor does it promise you understanding the wisdom of everything that befalls you; for some of it is deferred to a day when the files are disclosed. Nor does it promise you wealth or health as a price for faith; for worship is not a bargain with heaven. What it promises you is greater and firmer: the believer's whole affair is good for him, as the Prophet ﷺ said: "Wondrous is the affair of the believer, for his affairs are all good, and this applies to no one but the believer. If something good happens to him, he is grateful and that is good for him. If something bad happens to him, he bears it with patience and that is good for him" — narrated by Muslim (2999). And every fatigue, hardship, and anxiety that befalls the believer is an expiation and an elevation, as he ﷺ said: "No fatigue, nor disease, nor sorrow, nor sadness, nor hurt, nor distress befalls a Muslim, even if it were the prick he receives from a thorn, but that Allah expiates some of his sins for that" — narrated by al-Bukhari (5641) and Muslim (2573). So the promise is not "you will not suffer"; the promise is: "You will not suffer in vain, you will not suffer alone, and you will not suffer forever."

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Az-Zalzalah: 7–8] — a reference to the text of section 5.4. - Sahih Muslim (2999); and Sahih al-Bukhari (5641) and Sahih Muslim (2573) — with verification via Dorar al-Saniyyah (dorar.net).

Practise it yourself — a reckoning of three days: Try a miniature of the week's experiment proposed in section 5.7: three days only, and at the end of each day write down two lines: "What I did today for my purpose" and "What I did for my passing mood." Do not judge yourself while writing; only observe. At the end of the third day, compare the six lines and ask: which day was the calmest? This small sheet turns the question of purpose from an idea you read into a habit you have begun — and it is the practical bridge to the following units of the path.

The Day of Resurrection, on which people will be raised for reckoning and recompense
The smallest weight a human being can imagine — an allusion to the fact that no deed is lost, however small
Patience over affliction, seeking the reward with Allah, not in despair or negative surrender
Allah's placing things in their places with complete knowledge — its face may appear to the human being, or be delayed to the Last Day
Restraining the soul upon obedience to Allah, away from disobedience to Him, and over His painful decrees without discontent — and it is the believer's first stance at a calamity

7. Your Question

If it were true — hypothetically, for a single moment — that your life has a purpose written with a Creator who knows it, what is the first thing that would change in your day tomorrow? And what frightens you in this idea, and what reassures you in it? A writing task (completely private; it is not published and you are not asked to share it): Write for yourself, on one page: "What would change in my day if I were certain that my life has a purpose?" — and if you wish, you may discuss it optionally with your Companion in a live session; for some questions only ripen when they are said aloud. A performed practice task: Choose one trial you have passed through — small or great — and write about it in two lines in the order of section 5.9: At which chapter did its story stop in your feeling? And what can you not yet judge, because the book is not complete? This paper is reviewed with your Companion if you wish — not for you to receive a judgement on your pain, but for you to train yourself in reading your story as a reading of chapters, not a reading of the single page.

8. Mini-Quiz (Consolidation, Not Grades — Instant Feedback)

9. Your One Next Step

Performed practice task — “Take the step”

One step: Unit Four, "The Prophets and Messengers: One Chain and One Message" — for if this is the answer of purpose, how did it reach human beings? And how do you know it is really from Allah, not of human making? From there the story begins.

10. Companion Card

  • Ready discussion points: (1) What is the harshest picture of pain you have passed through or seen? And how does the answer "a house of testing" look before it — honestly, not in slogans? (2) What is the difference, for you, between "worship as a ritual" and "worship as a life"? (3) Which idea in the unit was the closest: purpose, Allah's freedom from need of us, or the justice of the Last Day?
  • Suggested follow-up questions: Did you write the reflection page? And do you want us to begin our meeting from it? Is there still a question about death or destiny that troubles you, which you want postponed to Unit 11 or opened now?
  • Warning signs and escalation: A crisis of meaning or visible signs of depression/despair ← the safe referral protocol immediately, before any content (Specification 2.4). Questions of "the problem of evil" in their complete philosophical form ← they are documented and directed to Unit 11 and the misconceptions team, and are not answered improvisationally. The Companion is forbidden here from direct preaching and from using the learner's pain as a "closing argument."
  • The piece's place in the weekly work plan: The reflection page (field 7) is reviewed — at the learner's request only — in the week's meeting, and upon it is built one small practical step of his choosing.
  • Additional items for the Companion Card: (1) The new performed practice task (reading the trial in the order of the chapters) is also reviewed — at the learner's request only, and by a strict rule: the Companion listens and helps with the reading, and never explains the learner's specific calamity by a determinate wisdom. (2) If section 5.8 opens a complete philosophical question of evil, the question is fixed in text and referred to Unit 11 and the misconceptions team, and is not completed improvisationally in the session.
