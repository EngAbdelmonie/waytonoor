Unit Ten — Women, Family and Society in Islam

WayToNoor — «طريق النور» — "Your way to the Light of Islam — with a companion for your first steps."

1. Opening Hook

The file most talked about Muslim women, and the file in which they are least heard from. Begin, then, from a scene described neither by opponents nor by supporters, but recorded by the Qur'an itself: an ordinary woman enters upon the Messenger of Allah, arguing with him about a matter in her family. He listens. Then Qur'an is revealed, to be recited until the Day of Resurrection, beginning with the words: "Certainly has Allah heard the speech of the one who argues with you." From this picture — not from the pictures of the media — read everything that follows.

2. Executive Summary

Women in the texts of Islam have one origin with men, one dignity and one responsibility before Allah, and rights established in the text since the seventh century: seeking knowledge, a completely independent financial liability and a bridal gift that is purely hers, and refusing a marriage she does not want. Hijab, according to the majority of scholars, is an obligation by Qur'anic text, presented here as it is, with neither softening nor exaggeration, within a reciprocal system of modesty that begins with the command to men to lower their gaze. Marriage is a covenant of tranquillity, affection and mercy; divorce is a disciplined last exit; and polygyny is a licence restricted by the condition of justice, not a general rule. And between the text and practice lies culture: whatever injustice you have seen in some country, its measure is the text itself — and the text is the woman's ally against custom, not her opponent.

3. Before You Begin: Prerequisites and Your Place in the Path

This is Unit 10 of 12. It came after the unit on ethics (9), which established the measure "the text is an ally of dignity"; this unit applies that measure to the most difficult social files and the most distorted in the media. It also stands alone for whoever comes to it from a direct search — one of the most common searches of all — and it is read more calmly after Units 2–5 for whoever follows the path in order.

4. Unit Objectives

By the end of this unit you will be able to: - Present women's place in the founding text (one soul, dignity through righteousness, and a complaint that was heard and changed a ruling). - Name women's rights established in the text: knowledge, independent money, the bridal gift, and refusing an unwanted marriage. - Summarise the text's position on hijab, divorce and polygyny as it is, with its evidence, with neither embellishment nor distortion. - Distinguish methodically between the ruling of the text and the practice of culture, and apply the measure to real examples. - Apply the "text × practice" measure to documented examples from named countries, and cite documented women's voices from the Prophetic era (such as Umm Salamah's counsel on the day of Al-Hudaybiyyah) in judging the general picture.

5. Main Text

5.1 Start Here: A Whole Surah Called "The Women", and a Woman Who Argued and Allah Heard Her

Open the Mushaf and you will find its fourth longest surah called "The Women" (An-Nisa). It begins with an address to all humanity: "O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from one soul and created from it its mate and dispersed from both of them many men and women" [An-Nisa: 1]. From one soul: one human origin for man and woman, one dignity, and one responsibility before Allah: "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" [Al-Hujurat: 13]. And confirming this equality in responsibility and reward, the Qur'an named men and women category by category in a single verse, sealing it with a single promise: "Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women… Allah has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward" [Al-Ahzab: 35]. And it said about the good life itself: "Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while he is a believer - We will surely cause him to live a good life, and We will surely give them their reward [in the Hereafter] according to the best of what they used to do" [An-Nahl: 97] — male or female, with no distinction in the promise or in the reward.

Open the Mushaf and you will find its fourth longest surah called "The Women" (An-Nisa).

Then hear this story from the heart of the Prophetic era: Khawlah bint Tha'labah came to the Prophet ﷺ arguing with him about her husband, who had pronounced zihar against her (a pre-Islamic formula that made a wife unlawfully forbidden to her husband in an unjust, suspended way). 'A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, was in the room, hearing some of her words and not hearing others. Then Allah revealed the opening of Surah Al-Mujadila: "Certainly has Allah heard the speech of the one who argues with you, [O Muhammad], concerning her husband and directs her complaint to Allah. And Allah hears your dialogue; indeed, Allah is Hearing and Seeing" [Al-Mujadila: 1] — and He abolished zihar and legislated an expiation for it. An ordinary woman argues with the Messenger of Allah about her home, and the Qur'an records her complaint as Qur'an to be recited until the Day of Resurrection, and changes the ruling for her sake. Keep this picture in mind as we go on; it is a fair measure for reading everything that follows.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nisa: 1], [Al-Hujurat: 13], [Al-Mujadila: 1], [Al-Ahzab: 35], [An-Nahl: 97]. - Sunan Abi Dawud (2214) and Ibn Majah (2063) — the story of Khawlah bint Tha'labah and the reason for the revelation of the opening of Al-Mujadila, authenticated by Al-Albani — verification via Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). - 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah, Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah (The Liberation of Women in the Era of the Message) — documenting women's participation in the Prophetic era.

5.2 Rights Established in the Text: Education, Money and Choice

In the seventh century CE, in a society some of whose members buried baby girls alive and regarded women as possessions to be inherited, the texts of Islam came to establish for women rights that were revolutionary by the standards of their age, and that are still disputed in modern societies:

In education: seeking knowledge in the texts is an address to all Muslims, men and women. The women of the Companions used to ask the Prophet ﷺ and requested a special day for their teaching, and he answered them — as in Sahih al-Bukhari (101) — and 'A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, was a scholarly reference to whom the senior Companions turned for jurisprudence and hadith after the death of the Prophet ﷺ. In money: a woman in Islam has a completely independent financial liability; she owns, sells, invests and gives charity, and her bridal gift is a right purely hers that neither husband nor father may touch: "And give the women [upon marriage] their [bridal] gifts graciously" [An-Nisa: 4]. Her maintenance is obligatory upon her husband, and that does not cancel her right to her own money, nor is she obliged by the Sharia to spend any of it on the home. In choosing a husband: it is not valid in Islam to force an adult, sane woman into a marriage she hates. The Prophet ﷺ said: "A previously married woman shall not be given in marriage until she is consulted, and a virgin shall not be given in marriage until her permission is sought" — narrated by Al-Bukhari (5136) and Muslim (1419). And the Prophet ﷺ annulled the marriage of a girl whose father had married her off while she disliked it, as in Sunan Abi Dawud and Ibn Majah from the hadith of Khansa' bint Khidham.

A contemporary scenario: a Muslim student at a Western university studying medicine, running a small project with her bridal gift and her savings, who refused a proposal that did not suit her, and her family respected her decision. This is not "a Muslim woman rebelling against her religion"; this is a Muslim woman practising rights her religion stipulated fourteen centuries ago. So if you see the opposite of that in some country, ask: is this from the text, or from custom?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nisa: 4, 19], [An-Nahl: 97], [Al-Ahzab: 35]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (101, 5136) and Sahih Muslim (1419) — women's seeking of knowledge and the hadith of consultation and permission — verification via Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). - Islam Q&A (islamqa.info) and Islamweb — the "Women's rights in Islam" files, with internal religious review.

5.3 Hijab Without Embellishment and Without Distortion: What Does the Text Actually Say?

Almost every seeker asks: why do Muslim women wear hijab? And is it religion or custom? The honest, brief answer: the majority of Muslim scholars across the centuries hold that a woman's covering of her hair and body — except the face and hands according to the majority of them — before unrelated men is a legal obligation by the text of the Qur'an: "And tell the believing women to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts and not expose their adornment except that which [necessarily] appears thereof and to wrap [a portion of] their headcovers over their chests" [An-Nur: 31], and "O Prophet, tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to bring down over themselves [part] of their outer garments" [Al-Ahzab: 59]. We present this as it is: an authoritative legal opinion with its evidence. We do not soften it to please anyone, and we do not exaggerate it; and for the precise jurisprudential details there are its trusted references, for our site is introductory and does not issue fatwas.

But the picture is not complete from one side only: the same passage began with a command to men first: "Tell the believing men to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts" [An-Nur: 30]. So modesty in Islam is a reciprocal system, not a burden on women alone. And hear, too, the testimony of those who wear it by choice — and they are millions of educated women, doctors and engineers in West and East — who say that for them it is identity, worship, and liberation from turning the body into a display window, not a symbol of oppression as it is portrayed. You may disagree with the idea, but fairness is to hear it from its wearers too, not only from its opponents.

A question for you: does women's freedom necessarily mean that their choices must resemble one single choice imposed by the dominant culture? Or does it mean that the culture respects her different choice, so long as it is her free choice?

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nur: 30–31], [Al-Ahzab: 59]. - Islam Q&A and Islamweb — the "Ruling on hijab and its evidence" files (presented in an introductory form with referral, not a personal fatwa). - Ibn Kathir, Tafsir al-Qur'an al-'Azim — at the interpretation of An-Nur 31 and Al-Ahzab 59.

5.4 Marriage: A Covenant of Affection and Mercy; Divorce: A Disciplined Exit, Not a Game

The Qur'an describes marriage in a phrase unmatched in its tenderness: "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought" [Ar-Rum: 21]. Tranquillity, affection and mercy: three words that sum up the philosophy of the family in Islam. It is a serious contract with the consent of both parties, witnesses and a bridal gift, and documented reciprocal rights: upon the husband are maintenance, care and living honourably — "And live with them in kindness" [An-Nisa: 19] — and upon both parties together are preserving the family and cooperating in raising the children. And the Prophet ﷺ said: "The best of you is the best of you to his family, and I am the best of you to my family" — narrated by At-Tirmidhi (3895) and Ibn Majah (1977), authenticated by Al-Albani. So the measure of a man's goodness with his Prophet is his treatment inside his home, not his image in gatherings.

If living together becomes impossible, Islam did not make the family an eternal prison, nor an open door for frivolity: it legislated divorce as a disciplined last exit, with stages, a waiting period, opportunities for reconciliation and arbitration from the families of both parties [Al-Baqarah: 229–232; An-Nisa: 35], and it permitted a woman to ransom herself through khul' if she hates staying with a hatred she cannot bear, as in the hadith of the wife of Thabit ibn Qays, whose basis is agreed upon in Sahih al-Bukhari (5273). As for polygyny, Islam did not invent it — it was widespread without limit in the societies of its age — rather, it restricted it to four and by the strict condition of justice: "But if you fear that you will not be just, then [marry only] one" [An-Nisa: 3]. So it made it a restricted licence for exceptional circumstances, not a general rule; and most Muslims in the world today are monogamous in reality and in law. We present this without emotional justification and without hiding; for you are a reader who deserves the complete picture.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [Ar-Rum: 21], [An-Nisa: 3, 19, 35], [Al-Baqarah: 229–232]. - Sahih al-Bukhari (5273) — the hadith of the wife of Thabit ibn Qays and khul'; and Sunan At-Tirmidhi (3895) — "The best of you is the best of you to his family" — verification via Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). - Islamweb — Encyclopaedia of the Muslim Family; and Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah by 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah.

5.5 Where Does Religion End and Culture Begin? The Million-Dollar Question

After all of the above, your legitimate question: if the texts are this clear, why do I see in Muslim countries practices that contradict them — prevention from education, forced marriage, and deprivation of inheritance?

The honest answer: because between the text and practice lie culture, customs, ignorance and people's interests, in every religion and every society. Some of these practices are in explicit violation of the texts you read a short while ago — inheritance itself is prescribed for women in a detailed Qur'anic text that admits no independent reasoning [An-Nisa: 11–12] — and some are tribal customs that preceded Islam in those countries and remained, unjustly, in its name. And the measure of fair judgement — which we commit to across this whole site — is that Islam is judged by its Qur'an, the authentic Sunnah of its Prophet and the biography of its first generation, not by the worst practices of its followers in any century; exactly as you do not judge medicine by the practices of a charlatan, nor law by a corrupt judge. And the paradox is that the strongest movements for reforming women's conditions inside Muslim societies themselves rest their greatest argument on these very texts: here, the text is the woman's ally against custom, not her opponent.

And society in the Islamic vision is wider than the family: a network of solidarity that begins with parents and kin, passes through the neighbour, the orphan and the needy, and widens until it includes even removing harm from the road as charity, and a kind word as charity, as in the agreed-upon hadiths of the "branches of faith". It is a network whose members the Qur'an describes in a comprehensive phrase: "The believing men and believing women are allies of one another" [At-Tawbah: 71] — men and women who are allies of one another in support and goodness, not opponents in a struggle. A cohesive family, a mutually supportive society, and a woman of honoured rights: this is the picture as the text presents it; and whatever contradicts it is to be presented to the text and criticised by it.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nisa: 11–12] on the prescribed inheritance shares, including women's textually prescribed share, [At-Tawbah: 71] on the believing men and women being allies of one another. - Sahih al-Bukhari (13) and Sahih Muslim (45) — "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself" on the foundation of solidarity — verification via Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). - 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah, Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah — the documented distinction between text and cultural practice. - Islam Q&A — the "Women in Islam" files, with internal religious review.

5.6 Children and the Extended Family: The Circle That Completes the Picture

The picture of the family in Islam is not completed by the couple alone; at its heart is a third generation: the children. Their rights among Muslims begin before their birth, with choosing a righteous mother and a righteous father, then follow one after another: a good name they will not be shamed by, nursing, care and tenderness documented in the texts, upbringing in faith and character by example before commands, and strict justice between siblings even in kisses and gifts — for the Prophet ﷺ refused the testimony of a father who wanted him to witness a gift he had singled out for one of his children over his siblings, and said to him: "Fear Allah and treat your children fairly", as in the agreed-upon hadith from the story of An-Nu'man ibn Bashir, may Allah be pleased with them both (Al-Bukhari 2587, Muslim 1623). And the Prophet ﷺ summarises the responsibility of the whole family in a single rule: "All of you are shepherds, and all of you are responsible for his flock" — agreed upon (Al-Bukhari 893, Muslim 1829). The father is a shepherd in his family and is responsible, the mother is a shepherd in her home and is responsible, and shepherding here is a trust one will be asked about on the Day of Resurrection, not an authority to boast of.

Then the circle widens to the extended family: grandparents, paternal and maternal uncles, whom kinship ties connect and whom difference of religion does not sever, as you saw in the verse of Luqman and the story of Asma' in earlier levels of this project. So the Muslim family is not an isolated island, but a link in a chain of generations. And with these three circles — a couple in affection and mercy, children with rights and care, and kinship maintained without break — Islam draws its first social model, upon which the rest of society is built. So whoever wants to understand "Muslim society", let him start here: from a home built on a covenant, not from grand slogans about the nation.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nisa: 1], [Ar-Rum: 21] — referral to the texts of sections 5.1 and 5.4. - Sahih al-Bukhari (2587, 893) and Sahih Muslim (1623, 1829) — the hadith of justice between children and the hadith "All of you are shepherds" — verification via Dorar Al-Saniyyah (dorar.net). - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — Encyclopaedia of the Muslim Family, chapters on children's rights and justice between them.

5.7 Women in the Wider Society: A Presence, Not a Margin

One last practical question remains: what about women's presence outside their homes — in knowledge, work and participation? The picture from the Prophetic era itself is wider than is usually told: women in the first community of Madinah attended the mosque, the prayer and the sermon; they asked, learned and gave opinions based on what they had learned — and 'A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, was, as we have seen, a reference for the senior Companions — and they participated in public life within the limits of modesty prescribed by the Sharia: in the markets, buying and selling; in the expeditions, dressing wounds, giving water and feeding; and in the migration and the pledge of allegiance, full partners in the founding event itself. 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah documented in his encyclopaedia Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah — with hundreds of referenced texts — a picture of this living presence in a detail that abbreviated headlines do not allow. So the textual origin is not "confinement" or isolation; the origin is disciplined participation, under the controls of mutual modesty between the two sexes that you met in the hijab section.

And upon this origin is built the picture of the contemporary Muslim woman you see in the universities of the Gulf, Cairo, Jakarta and London: a doctor, an engineer, a teacher, a researcher and a business owner, present in her society through her competence while at the same time preserving the limits of her religion in dress and mixing, as she understands them from its texts. You may see in these controls a restriction, and she may see in them a condition of safe presence that preserves her dignity from being reduced to her appearance. What matters methodically — and it is the measure of this whole unit — is that things be called by their correct names: this is the text and its field, and those are the cultures and their practices, varying in nearness to it and distance from it, and between the two, fair confusion is never permissible. And a methodological conclusion to this sensitive unit: we know that some of its readers will close it while some questions still pain them — about inheritance, or about jurisprudential details for which an introductory unit has no room, or about a harsh personal experience with particular Muslims, whose wound no text represents. To those we say clearly: your questions are not rejected, nor postponed forever; they are invited to the "Your Question" field in this very unit, where the audience's real questions are collected and answered in the unit's next edition with double religious review, and personal cases among them are referred to the people of knowledge with complete dignity and privacy. A unit that ends without an answer to your question is not the end of the dialogue; it is its documented beginning. And remember, before you close this page, the measure we began with: a surah called "The Women", and a woman who argued, and Allah heard her and changed the ruling for her sake. By these two pictures from the heart of the text itself, measure everything you have heard and will hear about women in Islam — from us and from others. Whatever agrees with them is from the text; and whatever contradicts them, look for its source in customs and desires, and you will usually find it there. With this measure alone you can walk through the noisiest file of our age with a calm compass that does not shake with every wave.

Sources: - 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah, Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah — chapters on women's participation in the society of the Prophetic era, with referenced texts. - Sahih al-Bukhari (101) — women's seeking of knowledge and the allocation of a day for them; referral to the text of section 5.2. - Islamweb (islamweb.net) — Encyclopaedia of the Muslim Family, materials on women's work and social participation.

5.8 Text × practice: four examples from named countries

Now apply the previous section's measure to real examples from named countries; for anonymous examples convince no one:

Saudi Arabia and women driving: For decades, women did not drive cars in Saudi Arabia, and the prohibition was long presented as "the position of the religion." Then the ban was lifted by an official decision in 2018 — and not a single letter of the Qur'an or the Sunnah changed between the prohibition and the permission. The methodological lesson: what changed was a state policy and a social custom, not a text; for much of what is presented to you as "Islam" is a human decision open to revision.

Afghanistan and girls' education: Since 2021, girls' secondary schools have been closed in Afghanistan, and among the most prominent objectors to this were major Islamic institutions: Al-Azhar Al-Sharif, the Muslim World League, and delegations of scholars of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, who travelled to Kabul carrying a single argument: the texts on seeking knowledge do not distinguish between male and female. So here the text does not stand watching the practice; it stands against it, on the tongues of its own scholars.

Depriving girls of inheritance in rural Egypt and the Levant: An old custom in rural regions: "the land is for the males," even at the expense of a detailed Qur'anic text that named the girls' shares [An-Nisa: 11]. Egyptian law intervened with an amendment in 2017 that criminalised withholding from an heir — woman or man — his or her legal share. So when you see a girl deprived of her inheritance, you are seeing a custom that contradicts the text and the law together; you are not seeing "Islam applied."

Forced marriage: In scattered tribal regions, families still marry off their daughters while they are minors or unwilling, and the hadith of seeking permission and consultation that you read in section 5.2 — and the Prophet's ﷺ annulment of the marriage of one who was married off while unwilling — stands directly against it; for it is a remnant of what preceded Islam, which the text came to correct, not to bless.

Four examples and a single measure: the text is fixed and available for you to read yourself, and the practices vary in nearness to it and distance from it, country by country. So do not judge the book by the cover of a country.

Sources: - The Noble Qur'an: [An-Nisa: 11] — referral to the text of section 5.5. - Documented, published facts: the lifting of the ban on women driving in Saudi Arabia (2018); the statements of Al-Azhar Al-Sharif, the Muslim World League, and the delegations of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation regarding girls' education in Afghanistan; and the Egyptian inheritance-law amendment (2017) criminalising the withholding of an heir's share.

5.9 Women's voices: from the Prophetic era to today

We close the unit with what we began with: that you hear from them themselves. These are documented women's voices from the Prophetic era, not commentaries about them:

Umm Salamah, may Allah be pleased with her — the counsel that changed the course of a historic event: On the day of Al-Hudaybiyyah, the Prophet ﷺ commanded his Companions to slaughter their sacrifices and shave their heads, to exit the state of ihram for the Umrah from which they had been turned back; but they were slow to move, out of the intensity of their grief and anger. He entered upon Umm Salamah, troubled, and she spoke her famous words, in their meaning: Go out and speak to no one, slaughter your sacrifice and shave; and when the people see you have done it, they will follow you. He ﷺ did so, and the people followed one after another in imitating him. Al-Bukhari narrated the story (2731). One woman, with a calm voice at a moment when an entire community was seething — that is "women's presence" in the text, not in slogans.

Khadijah, Sumayyah and Asma': The first to believe in him ﷺ was a woman — Khadijah, may Allah be pleased with her — who financed the call with her wealth and steadied his heart at the first revelation; and the first martyr in Islam was a woman — Sumayyah bint Khayyat — who stood firm under torture until death; and Asma' bint Abi Bakr used to carry food to the cave on the most dangerous nights of the Hijrah, and was nicknamed "Dhat an-Nitaqayn" (she of the two girdles). So the founding generation stood, at its greatest joints, on the shoulders of women, by name.

And today's voices: In its produced edition, this unit has a reserved place for real contemporary women's testimonies — new and long-standing Muslim women speaking in their own names and with their explicit permission about their own experiences, as the unit's hook promised you. [Reserved place — to be completed with real, authorised testimonies collected at production; and no testimony is ever worded on anyone's behalf] For a single invented testimony — however good its intention — would bring down the credibility of everything you have read in this unit; and the real, authorised voice alone is what befits its measure.

Sources: - Sahih al-Bukhari (2731) — the story of Al-Hudaybiyyah and the counsel of Umm Salamah, may Allah be pleased with her. - Safiur Rahman Mubarakpuri, Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum (The Sealed Nectar); and 'Abd al-Halim Abu Shaqqah, Tahrir al-Mar'ah fi 'Asr al-Risalah — Khadijah, Sumayyah and Asma', may Allah be pleased with them.

  • Warning signs and escalation: Mandatory double religious review before any referral or detailed answer in the matters of this unit (no individual independent reasoning by the companion, ever — a red line); when a woman seeker needs it or asks for it, female companionship is activated (an approved female companion joins the meetings) without embarrassment or justification required from her; painful personal experiences (coercion/domestic violence) ← received with complete listening and referred — when needed — to specialised support bodies, and her story is never used as content without explicit, revocable permission; precise questions of inheritance/lineage ← trusted fatwa references only.
  • The piece's place in the weekly work plan: Reviewed in the meeting: the audience question that was sent + the media/text comparison if written, and the collected questions are recorded in the register of the unit's next edition.
  • Published times: Reading the text about 10 minutes · Watching the video 7–9 minutes · Listening to the complete reading about 14 minutes (production estimates, as labelled in the Arabic edition).
